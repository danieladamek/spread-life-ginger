# Abstract

Ginger (*Zingiber officinale*) is read here through three records at once: the documents that describe its use, the human trials that test it, and the biology of the plant. The plant is a sterile, clonally propagated cultigen, unknown in the wild, whose genome was assembled in 2021 and whose key gingerol-making enzyme was demonstrated only in 2026 [1, 2]. Its pungent gingerols convert to shogaols on drying and heating, so fresh and dried ginger are different materials, a distinction Chinese medicine drew centuries before the chemistry [3, 4]. In people, the best-supported effect is on nausea in pregnancy; evidence for other uses is positive in direction and low in certainty, the motion-sickness trials conflict, and harm has not been detected in trials or a large cohort though regulators remain cautious [5, 6, 7]. After oral ginger the free compounds are not found in blood, which complicates every proposed mechanism [8]. The documentary record runs from Dioscorides and the *Suśruta Saṃhitā* through the medieval spice trade, the Caribbean plantations and the ginger-beer bottle to the "jake leg" poisoning of 1930 [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. This review is a snapshot as of 24 November 2026; most scientific sources were read as abstracts, and historical sources through scanned public-domain editions.

<!-- section: part-i -->
# Part I. Foundations

<!-- section: 1-why-three-records -->
## 1. Why ginger, and why three records at once

Ginger is unusual in being at once an ancient trade good, a kitchen staple, a regulated herbal medicine and a sequenced crop. Each of those identities has its own literature, and they seldom cite one another. A historian can say what a fourteenth-century cook did with powdered ginger, a trialist whether a capsule eased nausea, and a plant biologist which genes switch on in a swelling rhizome. None of the three can say alone whether the cook's dish contained what the trialist tested, or why the plant makes it in the first place. This review sets the records side by side and marks, section by section, where one supports, complicates or contradicts another. It reports what its sources say and where they stop, and it labels its own inferences as such. <!-- framing -->

<!-- section: 2-the-plant-in-brief -->
## 2. The plant in brief

The rest of this review depends on four facts about the plant: where it sits among its relatives, that it is propagated without seed, how its rhizome is built, and how large and unusual its genome is. <!-- framing -->

### A large tropical family

Ginger belongs to the Zingiberaceae, the largest family in the order Zingiberales, with 53 genera and over 1200 species across the tropics [14]. The family's classification was rebuilt in 2002 from DNA sequences, which showed that several of the floral and ovary traits used since 1889 had evolved more than once and that three of the four traditional tribes were not natural groups; the replacement recognises four subfamilies [14]. Within it, the genus *Zingiber* holds roughly 100 to 150 species in tropical and subtropical Asia, and comparisons of their complete chloroplast genomes now give the genus a solid backbone phylogeny [15]. *Zingiber officinale* is the type species of the whole family [16].

### A plant without seed

Cultivated ginger is described as a sterile cultigen, maintained entirely by planting pieces of rhizome [17, 18]. Sterility is not absolute. In 21 germplasm collections examined in India, pollen that took up stain ranged from 5.59% to 67.73% and pollen that germinated in vitro from 2.35% to 60.31%; the two most fertile collections turned out to be tetraploids with 2n = 44, against the normal 2n = 22 [19]. Chromosome studies in the 1960s had already found the cultivated clone carrying inversions and interchanges, the kind of structural rearrangement that disrupts pairing at meiosis [20], and a 2021 genome found two inversions larger than 15 Mb on chromosome 4 that its authors propose may be associated with ginger's infertility [1]. In a close relative that also propagates clonally, *Zingiber zerumbet*, the block has been located late in the process, at the point where the pollen tube should burst inside the ovule [21]. Flowering itself depends on day length in some cultivars, and short days favour swelling of the rhizome over growth of the shoot [22].

Clonal propagation has practical consequences. Every new plant carries whatever pathogens its parent piece carried, and multiplication of good stock is slow, which is why tissue-culture propagation was developed; micropropagated plants grown in a greenhouse did not differ chemically from conventionally propagated ones [18].

### The rhizome

What is sold as ginger "root" is a rhizome, a horizontal underground stem. Under light and electron microscopy it has an epidermis, a cortex and a central stele; its pericycle stays meristematic and keeps producing tissue inward [23]. Oil cells and oil cavities are scattered through it, the cavities forming by breakdown of cells, and as the rhizome matures its starch grains become fewer while oil cells and cavities multiply [23]. Imaging of intact rhizome sections by mass spectrometry finds the gingerols concentrated in the stele and cortex, while shogaols are spread more widely, including the epidermis [24].

### The genome

Three genome assemblies, each from a Chinese cultivar and each resolved into two haplotypes, were published between 2021 and 2024. The first reports haplotypes of 1.53 and 1.51 gigabases with 39,217 and 38,090 protein-coding genes, and about 62% of the genome made of transposable elements [16]. The second anchors a 3.1 Gb diploid assembly on 11 pseudochromosome pairs and predicts 73,006 gene models [1]. The third reports haplotypes of 1.55 and 1.44 Gb, retrotransposon bursts within the last million years, a recent whole-genome duplication and a lineage-specific expansion of genes for gingerol and related pathways [25]. Across cultivars the variation is real but modest: plants of *Z. officinale* from different geographical origins were genetically indistinguishable by the markers used in one study, differing chemically only in the quantities of their gingerols [26]. Most marker studies of cultivated ginger find little diversity, as would be expected of a crop multiplied by cuttings [27].

<!-- section: 3-the-chemistry-in-brief -->
## 3. The chemistry in brief

Two groups of compounds carry most of what people notice about ginger: the pungent phenolics and the aromatic volatile oil. A third, a protein-digesting enzyme, matters in the kitchen. This section fixes the vocabulary; sections 12 and 15 develop it. <!-- framing -->

### The pungent principles

The pungent principle had a name long before it had a structure. Analyses of commercial gingers by the pharmacist J. C. Thresh, reported from 1879, already separated a "gingerol", a viscid, straw-coloured, odourless and extremely pungent fluid, from a volatile oil that was aromatic but not pungent, and found that fine Jamaica ginger carried only about half the oil of Cochin and African samples [28]. The structure of the pungent principles was settled in 1969. Acetone extracts of dried rhizome contain a series of gingerols with side chains of different lengths, while shogaol and zingerone, earlier described as natural constituents, were absent from the rhizome and formed only when gingerol or the oleoresin was treated with heat or alkali [3]. Later work confirmed the point for fresh ginger: in fresh rhizomes from 17 Australian clones, 6-gingerol was the major pungent compound, 8- and 10-gingerol occurred at lower levels, and shogaols were not detected in extracts made at room temperature [17]. A recent review groups the wider family, defined by a shared 3-methoxy-4-hydroxyphenyl unit, into gingerols, shogaols, paradols, zingerone, gingerdiones and gingerdiols, and attributes their pungency to activation of the TRPV1 receptor [29].

<!-- figure: fig-drying -->

The gingerol molecule is unstable because of a β-hydroxy ketone group. In water it loses a molecule of water to become the corresponding shogaol, and the reaction is reversible: gingerol and shogaol interconvert, fastest at high temperature and low pH and slowest at pH 4 [30]. That chemistry is why fresh and dried ginger differ, and section 15 follows its consequences into the kitchen.

### The aromatic oil

The aroma comes from the volatile oil, a mixture of terpenoids [31]. Its major sesquiterpenes, α-zingiberene and β-sesquiphellandrene, are made by a single enzyme that ginger shares with turmeric [31]. The citrus note of fresh ginger comes from monoterpenes, above all geraniol and its derivatives geranial, geranyl acetate and citronellol; a ginger enzyme that oxidises geraniol to citral has been cloned and characterised [32]. Drying changes this fraction too, raising terpene hydrocarbons and converting some monoterpene alcohols to acetates [33], and oil yield and composition differ between cultivars and drying methods [34].

### The enzyme

Ginger rhizome also contains a protease, named zingibain in 1973, that digests collagen far more readily than muscle protein, works best around 60 °C and is rapidly inactivated at 70 °C [35]. Partly purified, the same activity clots milk [36].

### How variable the material is

Ginger products vary widely in what they contain. Across nine ginger dietary supplements, the content of individual gingerols and of 6-shogaol varied with coefficients of variation between 45.7% and 141.7% [37]. Plants from different origins carry the same compounds in different amounts [26], and one Australian cultivar known as "Jamaican" had the highest gingerol content and was the most pungent of seventeen clones [17]. A trial that reports "1 g of ginger" without saying which ginger therefore says less than it seems to. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

# Part II. History and lore

<!-- section: 4-origin-and-dispersal -->
## 4. Origin and dispersal

Ginger's origin cannot be settled the usual way, by finding its wild populations, because none is known. What follows is an account of how the question has been argued over 150 years, and of where the arguments now stand. <!-- framing -->

### No wild ginger

Every authority read for this review agrees on the starting fact. The great pharmacological histories of the nineteenth century describe ginger as native to Asia, "universally cultivated, but not known in a wild state" [11], and as cultivated in India from prehistoric times but not then known wild [38]; an American dispensatory of 1898 gives its native country as unknown [39]. The botanist J. D. Hooker, quoted in 1903, put the problem more sharply: he knew of no botanist who had found it wild, and suspected that no one had looked [9]. Modern botany says the same thing in its own terms: ginger is unknown in the wild and has been propagated from rhizomes for millennia [40].

### Three accounts

Three accounts of where it was domesticated are current, and the sources read do not reconcile them. A 2011 review of ginger's genetic resources places its origin in the Indo-Malayan region and its domestication in India and China, which it treats as the centre of origin of the species [27]. A 2021 genome paper states instead that ginger was first cultivated and used by Austronesian peoples more than 3000 years ago and reached South India with the Austronesian expansion [1]; that sentence sits in the paper's introduction as background, and the searches for this review found no primary study that tests it. A Chinese tradition recorded in the early twentieth century reads the written character for ginger as pointing to an origin outside China, on the Mongolian borders [4], which neither modern account supports. <!-- synthesis -->

The genetic evidence is thin because the crop is clonal. Plants of *Z. officinale* from different geographical origins were indistinguishable by the DNA markers used in one study [26], and marker studies in general find little diversity in the cultivated crop [27]. What the genome may add is the history of the crop's sterility: the cultivated clone already carried chromosomal inversions and interchanges when it was examined in 1969 [20], and the 2021 genome found large inversions it links to infertility [1]. <!-- framing -->

### The names

The word has travelled further than the plant's documented history. English "ginger" comes, through medieval Latin and Greek, from Arabic *zanjabīl* [9]. Sanskrit *śṛṅgavera* is usually glossed "antlered", from the shape of the rhizome [38], but the 1903 editors of *Hobson-Jobson* thought that meaning an "imaginary etymology" laid over a borrowed word, and proposed the Malayalam *inchi* (from which *inchi-ver*, "ginger root") as the deeper source [9]. Persian intermediaries are proposed for the Greek form [38]. Arab geographers, misled by the sound of *zanjabīl*, sometimes placed ginger on the coast of Zinj in East Africa [9]. Sanskrit also gave ginger titles rather than names: *mahauṣadha*, "great remedy", and *viśvabheṣaja*, "universal medicine" [38].

<!-- section: 5-the-old-world-record -->
## 5. The old-world record

By the first centuries of the common era ginger was traded across the Indian Ocean, written into Greek, Indian and Chinese medicine, and eaten at the table of at least one famous diner. This section follows the documentary record; its gaps are named at the end. <!-- framing -->

<!-- figure: fig-timeline -->

### The archaeology

The earliest physical evidence read for this review comes from Oc Eo in southern Vietnam, a trading port of the early centuries CE, where starch and other microscopic plant remains on grinding stones of South Asian type were identified as ginger, turmeric, fingerroot, sand ginger, galangal, clove, nutmeg and cinnamon, the ingredients of a curry [41].

### Greece and Rome

The Greek physician Dioscorides, writing about AD 65, described ginger as a plant produced mostly in "Troglodytic Arabia" on the Red Sea, where the green plant was boiled and mixed into drinks and stews, and its small whitish roots were peppery to taste and smell [9]. He classed it as warming, digestive, gently laxative and good for the stomach, an ingredient in eye-salves and antidotes [38]. A tenth-century Byzantine copy of his herbal illustrates the plant under the name *zengiberis* [42]. Pliny, a few years later, mentioned it briefly in his chapter on pepper: it grows in Arabia and "Troglodytica", is "of intense pungency", decays quickly, and sells at Rome for six denarii a pound [43]. That both writers placed ginger in Arabia reflects the route, not the origin: the Romans received it through the Red Sea ports, and in the second century ginger was among the Indian goods on which duty was levied at Alexandria [11]. Roman cookery used it in measured mixtures, among them a spiced salt credited with aiding digestion and warding off disease, and a digestive relish of cumin, ginger, rue, dates, pepper and honey taken with lettuce [44].

### India

In Indian medicine ginger is fundamental. The *Suśruta Saṃhitā* groups it with long pepper and black pepper as *trikaṭu*, "the three pungents", credited with destroying fat and phlegm and curing catarrh, dullness of appetite and indigestion [10]. The later Sanskrit lexicons distinguish fresh ginger (*ārdraka*) from dried (*śuṇṭhī*) and describe it as acrid and digestive, used against cold humours, nausea, cough, asthma and colic [38]. A compilation of the Sanskrit medical works published in Calcutta in 1877 adds the household detail: ginger with salt before meals as a carminative said to clear the tongue and throat and sharpen the appetite, the fresh juice with honey for catarrh and cough, ginger juice in milk as a snuff for headache, and compound preparations such as *samaśarkarā cūrṇa*, six spices in rising proportions, dried ginger the largest, with an equal weight of sugar, for dyspepsia and poor appetite [45]. A recent Ayurvedic review traces the same uses through the classical texts to formulations still in use [46].

### China

Confucius, according to the *Analects*, "was never without ginger when he ate" [47]. Chinese materia medica treated fresh ginger (*shēngjiāng*) and dried ginger (*gānjiāng*) as different drugs; Li Shizhen's sixteenth-century *Bencao gangmu* gives them separate articles [4]. An English summary of that tradition records fresh ginger as used to stimulate digestion, quiet nausea and check cough, and as thought to counteract mushroom poisoning, and dried ginger for a wider range including haemorrhage [4]. A recent review of the tradition distinguishes fresh, dried, baked and carbonised ginger, each credited with different strengths, while noting that their main chemical constituents are nearly identical [48].

### The Islamic world

The Qur'an names *zanjabīl* among the drinks of paradise, a cup "mixed with the water of Zenjebil" in George Sale's eighteenth-century translation [9]. Galen recommended ginger for complaints arising from cold humours, and Ibn Sina and the Arabic and Persian physicians followed the Greeks while enlarging on its reputation as an aphrodisiac [38].

### What this section could not consult

Three bodies of evidence that belong here were not read directly: Dioscorides, Galen and the Arabic pharmacologists in full modern editions, the Sanskrit texts beyond one translation and one nineteenth-century compilation, and the Chinese classical formularies. They are cited here only through the nineteenth- and early twentieth-century scholars who read them, and the section should be taken as a guide to them rather than a substitute. <!-- framing -->

<!-- section: 6-europe-to-the-americas -->
## 6. Europe to the Americas

In medieval Europe ginger was a luxury, a medicine and a seasoning at once; in the sixteenth century it crossed the Atlantic; in the nineteenth it became a drink; and in 1930 its name became attached to a mass poisoning. <!-- framing -->

### The medieval trade

Ginger reached England early: it is named in Anglo-Saxon leechbooks of the eleventh century [11]. It appears in customs tariffs at Acre about 1173, Barcelona in 1221, Marseilles in 1228 and Paris in 1296, and in thirteenth- and fourteenth-century England it was, after pepper, the commonest spice, at an average of nearly 1s. 7d. a pound, "about the price of a sheep" [11]. Italian merchants of the fourteenth century knew three grades by their origin: *belledi* (from the Arabic for "country", common ginger), *colombino* (from Quilon on the Malabar coast) and *micchino* (by way of Mecca) [11]. A Parisian household book of about 1393 prices them: a pound of colombin ginger powder at eleven sous, a quarter-pound of Mecca ginger at five, the Mecca being whiter, softer and dearer [49]. Marco Polo saw ginger growing in China and India, and named the Quilon kind [9, 11]. Archaeology now confirms the documents: ginger was among the spices recovered from *Gribshunden*, the Danish royal flagship that sank in 1495 on its way to a political summit [50].

### Ginger in humoral medicine

The reasoning behind the medieval demand is set out in the pharmacology texts of the period. Health was a balance of four humours, each hot or cold and wet or dry; ginger, classed as hot and dry, was used to correct a cold and wet constitution and diseases attributed to cold [51]. The classification was precise: an Irish materia medica rates ginger hot in the third degree and wet in the second [52], Gerard's 1597 *Herball* hot and dry in the third degree, with candied green ginger moister and reputed to provoke desire [53]. The degrees were not fixed: Culpeper's herbal, in its 1835 edition, puts ginger hot and dry only in the second degree, crediting it with helping digestion, warming the stomach and expelling wind, and repeatedly adds a little ginger to purging herbs so that "the stomach will not loath it" [54]. That use of ginger to stop a medicine causing nausea is an early form of the use the modern trials support best. <!-- synthesis --> Imported spices were valued above cheap local remedies, and partly for being exotic [51]. The same logic shaped demand: preferences for ginger differed between northern and southern Europe in ways that reflected humoral principles [12], and a French physician's treatise of 1702, translated into English in 1745, still judged it good for the old, the phlegmatic and the slow to digest, and "pernicious" to the young of hot and bilious constitution [55].

### Across the Atlantic

Ginger was carried to the Americas in the sixteenth century; the nineteenth-century account credits Francisco de Mendoza with taking it from the East Indies to New Spain [11]. It flourished on Hispaniola and Puerto Rico in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, found more buyers in England and the Low Countries than in Castile, and fitted poorly into the Spanish fleet system, which encouraged privateering and contraband [12]. Gerard reported that English raiders at Santo Domingo dug it up growing wild [53]. The shift was already visible in 1596, when the Dutch traveller Linschoten wrote that most of the ginger reaching Spain came from the Cape Verde islands, São Tomé, Brazil and Santo Domingo, and little from Portuguese India, whose ginger he judged better but too costly to ship [28]. By the eighteenth century fresh ginger was eaten in the Antilles as a salad, and preserved ginger was carried at sea against scurvy [55]. Jamaica ginger became the grade most esteemed in the London market [11, 39], and scraped ginger was often bleached with the fumes of burning sulphur or with chlorinated lime, so that some of it looked whitewashed [28]; edible ginger varieties are recorded as transplanted to the Caribbean by Spanish colonists in the sixteenth century [56].

### From spice to drink

Ginger beer, ginger wine and ginger ale became a large part of the English mineral-water trade in the nineteenth century, and bottled ginger ale from Belfast was a fashionable drink across the United States by 1888 [38]. The household ginger beer of the period was often fermented with a gelatinous "ginger-beer plant", which the botanist Marshall Ward showed in 1891 to be a symbiotic association of a new yeast and a new bacterium, comparable to a lichen [57].

### "Jake"

The episode most often associated with ginger in American history was not caused by ginger. In 1930, during Prohibition, an illicit extract of Jamaica ginger, drunk to get around the Prohibition laws, was adulterated with tri-ortho-cresyl phosphate [13]. Thousands were poisoned [58]; one account puts the number with partial paralysis at 50,000 [59]. The damage to the spinal cord and nerves was only partly reversible, and survivors examined 47 years later still showed spasticity [58]. The gait it left, "jake leg", was sung about on a dozen commercial recordings by Southern musicians [13]. The neurotoxicity of the compound had been recognised in France before the epidemic, and the cause was identified by an American pharmacologist and chemist soon after it [60].

<!-- section: 7-why-people-spice-food -->
## 7. Why people spice food

One influential answer to why cuisines use spices at all comes from evolutionary biology, and ginger is part of the dataset behind it. <!-- framing -->

The antimicrobial hypothesis holds that spices are used because they inhibit or kill food-spoilage microorganisms. Its authors tabulated 43 spices in 4578 meat-based recipes from 93 traditional cookbooks covering 36 countries, and found that as mean annual temperature rose, so did the proportion of recipes containing spices, the number of spices per recipe and the use of the most potent antibacterial spices [61]. They considered four alternatives (that spices supply nutrients, disguise spoilage, promote cooling by sweating, or do nothing useful) and found none well supported [61, 62]. A recent review of ginger in British food history argues against the masking explanation in particular, and traces ginger's disappearance from savoury English cooking after the seventeenth century, as spiced food went out of fashion, while it survived in sweet baking [63].

Ginger's compounds do inhibit microbes in the laboratory (section 13), which fits the hypothesis. The medieval sources of section 6 give a different stated reason for spicing food, the balancing of humours, and the Roman spiced salts were credited with aiding digestion [44]. The reasons people give and the reasons a practice persists need not be the same, and a correlation across cookbooks cannot separate them. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

# Part III. Health: the human evidence

<!-- section: 8-nausea-and-vomiting -->
## 8. Nausea and vomiting

Nausea is the use for which ginger has been tested longest and most often, and the answer depends on the cause of the nausea. This section takes motion, pregnancy, surgery and chemotherapy in turn, in the order in which they were first studied. Throughout, nausea and vomiting are kept apart, because ginger often moves one and not the other, and any amount of ginger mentioned is the test article of a study, not a recommendation. <!-- framing -->

<!-- figure: fig-evidence -->

### Motion sickness: where the trials began

The modern clinical literature on ginger starts with a 1982 letter in *The Lancet*. In a double-blind comparison, students who were highly susceptible to motion sickness took powdered ginger, dimenhydrinate or placebo before sitting in a rotating chair; a later tabulation of the study reports that those given ginger lasted longest in the chair [64, 65]. The result was striking enough to start a line of replication.

The replications divided. On the open sea, 80 naval cadets unaccustomed to heavy weather took 1 g of powdered rhizome or placebo; ginger reduced vomiting and cold sweating significantly, but its effects on nausea and vertigo did not reach significance [66]. In the laboratory the effect did not hold. A NASA-supported study found three doses of ginger no better than placebo, while dimenhydrinate, promethazine and scopolamine protected [67]. A second rotating-chair study with 28 volunteers found that neither powdered nor fresh ginger gave any protection, and that powdered ginger did not change gastric emptying [68]. A German study recorded eye movements under vestibular and visual stimulation and found ginger no different from placebo, whereas dimenhydrinate damped the response; its authors concluded that ginger does not act on the balance system in the way conventional motion-sickness drugs do and that any benefit more likely arises in the stomach [69]. The one later mechanistic study, using a rotating visual field in thirteen volunteers, found that ginger reduced nausea along with abnormal gastric rhythm and the rise in vasopressin [70].

The European regulator nonetheless accepts powdered ginger for preventing nausea and vomiting in motion sickness as a well-established use, the only well-established use in its monograph, and limits it to adults [7]. A 2005 review read the same trials more sceptically and found the motion-sickness evidence unconvincing [71]. The two positions rest on the same handful of small studies weighed differently. <!-- synthesis -->

### Pregnancy

Pregnancy is where ginger's evidence is strongest. The first randomised trial, in 1991, was in hyperemesis gravidarum, the severe form of pregnancy sickness: 27 women completed a double-blind crossover of powdered ginger against placebo, 19 of them preferred the ginger period, and relief scores were significantly better with ginger [72]. A ten-year run of trials followed. In 70 women at or before 17 weeks, ginger reduced both nausea scores and vomiting episodes over four days compared with placebo [73]. In 120 women given a standardised extract, nausea fell from the first day of treatment and retching less so, with no significant effect on vomiting [74]. In an equivalence trial in 291 women, ginger and vitamin B6 reduced nausea, retching and vomiting to an equivalent extent over three weeks [75].

The pooled analyses agree on nausea and part company on vomiting. A 2005 systematic review of six double-blind trials with 675 participants found four favouring ginger over placebo and two showing it as effective as vitamin B6 [76]. A 2014 meta-analysis of twelve trials involving 1278 women found ginger significantly better than placebo for nausea but not for the number of vomiting episodes [77]. The 2022 umbrella review ranked the antiemetic effect in pregnancy as its best-supported finding, with a large effect and a high GRADE rating [5]. Two later appraisals are more cautious: a 2024 umbrella review rated the seven meta-analyses it found, which drew on 22 overlapping studies, as of critically low to low quality [78], and the Cochrane review of all interventions for early-pregnancy nausea judged the evidence for every intervention, ginger included, to be limited and of low quality [79].

### Surgery

The surgical trials divide early and stay divided. In 60 women having major gynaecological surgery, ginger produced fewer recorded episodes of nausea than placebo, similar to metoclopramide [80]. In 108 women having laparoscopic gynaecological surgery, ginger at two doses was no better than placebo, and nausea and vomiting rose slightly with dose [81]. The first systematic review, in 2000, found the pooled reduction in postoperative nausea not significant [82]; a 2006 meta-analysis of five trials with 363 patients found relative risks of 0.69 for nausea and vomiting and 0.61 for vomiting, favouring ginger [83]. In 239 women having caesarean section under spinal anaesthesia, ginger reduced the number of nausea episodes during surgery but did not reduce the proportion of women who had nausea [84].

### Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy has the largest trials and the least consistent results. In 60 chemotherapy cycles in children and young adults with bone sarcoma, all given standard antiemetics, ginger capsules reduced moderate to severe acute nausea from 93.3% of cycles to 55.6%, with similar reductions in vomiting and in delayed symptoms [85]. In 576 adults analysed in a multicentre trial, all ginger doses reduced the severity of acute nausea on the first day [86]. A trial in 162 adults focused on delayed symptoms found no difference from placebo in any primary or secondary measure [87]. A 2023 trial of a standardised extract in 103 adults found benefit in nausea-related quality of life and delayed nausea, and none in acute symptoms [88]. The reviews mirror the trials: a 2013 review of seven studies counted three positive, two positive with caveats and two negative [89], and a 2022 meta-analysis of 23 trials found no overall difference in the incidence of acute or delayed nausea or vomiting, with a reduction in acute vomiting only in one dosing subgroup [90].

### What the trials share

The weaknesses are common to all four settings. Of 109 randomised trials reviewed in 2020, only 43 (39.4%) were judged to provide high-quality evidence, with small samples and unstandardised outcome measures as the usual faults [91]. The test articles vary from fresh rhizome to powdered root to standardised extracts, and few trials report how much of any compound they delivered. <!-- framing -->

<!-- section: 9-pain-inflammation-and-metabolic-outcomes -->
## 9. Pain, inflammation and metabolic outcomes

The second large body of trials tests ginger as an anti-inflammatory, an idea with roots in the laboratory work of the 1970s and 1980s described in section 11. The pattern of this section is consistent: pooled effects usually favour ginger, and the certainty attached to them is usually low. <!-- framing -->

### Osteoarthritis

Two trials from 2000 and 2001 set the terms. A Danish double-blind crossover compared a ginger extract with ibuprofen and placebo in osteoarthritis of the hip or knee; ibuprofen ranked above ginger, which ranked above placebo, but a significant effect of ginger could be shown only with exploratory statistics in the first treatment period [92]. A six-week American trial in 261 patients with knee osteoarthritis found that 63% of the extract group and 50% of the placebo group met its pain-response criterion, an effect its authors called moderate; the extract combined *Zingiber officinale* with *Alpinia galanga*, and gastrointestinal side effects, mostly mild, were reported by 59 patients on the extract against 21 on placebo [93]. A 2015 meta-analysis of five placebo-controlled trials with 593 patients found a small but significant reduction in pain (standardised mean difference −0.30) and noted that most trials lacked an adequate intention-to-treat analysis [94]. The 2022 umbrella review rates the effect as small, with high certainty [5].

### Other pain

A 2011 systematic review of eight trials with 481 participants found tentative support for an effect on pain and judged the evidence insufficient overall [95]. Muscle soreness after eccentric exercise was reduced by about a quarter, compared with placebo, by both raw and heat-treated ginger taken for eleven days, in two trials of 34 and 40 volunteers [96]. For period pain, a 2009 trial in 150 students found ginger as effective as mefenamic acid and ibuprofen [97]; meta-analyses in 2015 and 2024 found ginger better than placebo for pain intensity [98, 99], the later one with high heterogeneity, sparse reporting of safety and no difference from non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [99]. For migraine there is one double-blind trial, in which sixty adults given ginger extract on top of intravenous ketoprofen in an emergency room responded better at one, one and a half and two hours [100].

### Blood sugar, lipids and inflammatory markers

Most metabolic trials measure laboratory values over weeks. Two meta-analyses in type 2 diabetes, each of ten trials, report lower fasting glucose and glycated haemoglobin, with a pooled HbA1c difference of −1.00 in one [101] and −0.57 in the other, where heterogeneity was 88.6% [102]. They disagree about lipids: the first reports improvement [101], the second no significant effect on any lipid fraction [102]. An older trial in patients with coronary disease found no change in lipids or blood sugar after three months of powdered ginger [103]. For inflammatory markers, a 2025 dose–response meta-analysis of 29 trials reports reductions in C-reactive protein, tumour necrosis factor α and interleukin-6, with high heterogeneity [104]; a 2026 umbrella review of 36 publications reaches a favourable overall verdict [105]; and a 2025 review of meta-analyses agrees on direction while pointing to heterogeneity of 98.1% in one analysis and possible bias in blinding in another [106]. The umbrella review grades the glycaemic effect anywhere from none to very large at very low to moderate certainty, and the lipid effect as small at very low certainty [5].

None of the sources read reports an effect of ginger on a disease outcome, such as a diabetic complication, a heart attack or a joint replacement; the evidence concerns symptoms and surrogate measures over weeks to months. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

<!-- section: 10-safety-interactions-and-pregnancy -->
## 10. Safety, interactions and pregnancy

Ginger is a food, and evidence about harm comes mostly from trials designed to detect benefit and from observational studies of people who chose to use it. This section reports what has been looked for and not found, which is different from what has been ruled out. <!-- framing -->

### What the regulator concluded

The European Union monograph, revised in 2025, is the most formal assessment available. It lists common gastrointestinal effects (stomach upset, belching, indigestion, heartburn and nausea) and hypersensitivity of unknown frequency, records no known interactions, and has no reported cases of overdose [7]. It sets age limits for every use, and it does not recommend the well-established motion-sickness use under 18 [7].

### Pregnancy

The trials did not detect harm: no adverse effect on pregnancy outcome in the 70-woman trial [73], normal birthweights, gestational ages and malformation rates in the 120-woman extract trial [74], and no significant risk of miscarriage in the 2014 meta-analysis, though with a confidence interval from 0.65 to 15.11 that is too wide to exclude one [77]. Observational studies extend the numbers. A Canadian cohort followed 187 pregnancies exposed to ginger in the first trimester and found no difference from a comparison group in malformations or other outcomes [107]. The Norwegian Mother and Child Cohort compared 1020 women who reported using ginger with more than 67,000 who did not and found no increased risk of malformations, stillbirth or perinatal death, preterm birth, low birthweight or low Apgar score [6].

Regulators have read the same evidence conservatively. The European monograph describes a moderate amount of data, 300 to 1000 pregnancy outcomes, showing no malformative or toxic effect on the fetus, notes that animal data on reproductive toxicity are insufficient, and advises as a precaution that use in pregnancy is preferably avoided; it does not recommend use while breast-feeding, for lack of data [7].

### Bleeding and anticoagulants

The concern began in the laboratory and in small studies of platelets. Seven Danish women who ate 5 g of raw ginger a day for a week showed a fall in serum thromboxane [108], and in human blood in vitro several ginger constituents inhibited platelet activation more potently than aspirin [109]. Studies in people taking dried ginger did not reproduce an effect: 2 g had no effect on bleeding time or platelet function in eight healthy men [110], and 4 g daily for three months did not alter platelet aggregation in patients with coronary disease, though a single 10 g dose did [103]. In twelve healthy men, ginger at recommended doses did not change clotting or the handling of warfarin [111]. Against that, a prospective study of 171 patients on warfarin found ginger among the complementary products associated with self-reported bleeding [112]. A 2000 review found no evidence of a warfarin–ginger interaction in the literature to that date [113], and the regulator lists none [7]. The evidence is reassuring for dried ginger in healthy people and thin for people on long-term anticoagulation, the group in which a small effect would matter. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

<!-- section: 11-how-it-might-work-and-the-bioavailability-problem -->
## 11. How it might work, and the bioavailability problem

Mechanisms for ginger's effects have been proposed for fifty years, nearly all from cells, isolated tissue or animals. Whether they operate in a person depends on how much of which compound reaches which tissue, and that is where the account becomes difficult. <!-- framing -->

### The anti-inflammatory chemistry

The anti-inflammatory idea rests on enzyme work. Ginger's inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis was described in the early 1970s and repeatedly confirmed, and was later extended to leukotriene synthesis through 5-lipoxygenase, a combination that distinguishes it from ordinary non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [114]. Gingerols and related diarylheptanoids were identified as the active inhibitors of both enzyme pathways in the 1980s and early 1990s [115].

### The gut and serotonin receptors

For nausea, the best-supported mechanism is inhibition of the 5-HT3 receptor, the target of standard antiemetic drugs. In 2006, 6-, 8- and 10-gingerol and 6-shogaol all inhibited 5-HT3 receptor activity in cultured cells and contractions of guinea-pig gut [116]. In 2013 ginger extracts, 6-gingerol and 6-shogaol inhibited human 5-HT3 receptors, in cells and in human gut nerve tissue, at low micromolar concentrations and by a non-competitive mechanism, binding elsewhere than the drugs do [117]. In guinea-pig tissue the effect on 5-HT3 and muscarinic M3 receptors was weak, and 5-HT4 receptors appeared not to be involved [118]. Ginger compounds also hastened the movement of a test meal through the gut in mice, comparably to metoclopramide [119].

Human studies point to the stomach. Ginger capsules shortened gastric half-emptying time from 26.7 to 13.1 minutes in 24 healthy volunteers [120] and from 16.1 to 12.3 minutes in 11 patients with functional dyspepsia [121], with no change in symptoms in either study. The motion-sickness work points the same way, finding no effect on the balance system [69].

<!-- figure: fig-gastric -->

### Pungency and TRPV1

Gingerols were identified in 2002 as a new class of agonists at the capsaicin receptor TRPV1, with potency rising with the length of the side chain [122]. Later work found shogaols more potent than gingerols at the receptor, and 10-shogaol active without being pungent [123], and showed that gingerol, shogaol and zingerone depend on the same two residues in the channel that capsaicin does [124].

### What reaches the blood

The first human pharmacokinetic study gave 27 volunteers single doses from 100 mg to 2.0 g. No participant had detectable free 6-, 8- or 10-gingerol or 6-shogaol in plasma; they appeared as glucuronide and sulfate conjugates, peaked at about an hour and were cleared with half-lives under two hours [8]. A study in asthma patients taking ginger extract for 56 days found about one-third of 6-gingerol reduced to gingerdiols and more than 90% of 6-shogaol converted to other metabolites [125]. Laboratory and mouse work traces the low exposure to rapid first-pass conjugation [126].

<!-- figure: fig-cmax -->

The receptor studies use micromolar concentrations of the free compounds; the human studies find conjugates and metabolites in blood. Two readings fit the evidence. Ginger may act mainly on the stomach and gut wall, which meet the free compounds before the liver does; or the metabolites may themselves be active. The sources read here do not settle which. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

### Beyond the gut

Laboratory studies attribute many other activities to ginger, among them inhibition of *Helicobacter pylori* at microgram-per-millilitre concentrations [127] and antioxidant and anticancer effects in cells and animals [128]. By the scope of this review these are noted, not argued: none has a body of human trials behind it. <!-- framing -->

# Part IV. The plant itself

<!-- section: 12-the-genome-and-the-making-of-gingerols -->
## 12. The making of gingerols

How ginger builds its pungent compounds has been worked out over fifty years by three different methods: feeding labelled precursors to the plant, measuring enzyme activities and gene expression, and finally expressing candidate genes in microbes. Each stage confirmed the outline of the one before. <!-- framing -->

### The outline from labelled precursors, 1976–1981

The first pathway came from feeding isotope-labelled compounds to whole plants and rhizome slices. In 1976 the evidence pointed to dihydroferulate, built from the amino acid phenylalanine, condensing with malonate and hexanoate in what the authors called a "biological Claisen" reaction, followed by reduction of the product to 6-gingerol [129]. The fuller study refined it: phenylalanine is made into ferulic acid, which condenses with malonate and hexanoate to give 6-dehydrogingerdione; hexanoate goes in intact; and the dione is reduced in two steps to 6-gingerol [130]. The length of the gingerol side chain, and so the difference between 6-, 8- and 10-gingerol, follows from which fatty acid joins the condensation. <!-- synthesis -->

### Enzymes and genes, 2006–2018

Enzyme assays then confirmed the front of the pathway. Crude extracts of ginger and turmeric leaf, shoot and rhizome carried the activities of the phenylpropanoid enzymes, but in ginger a thioesterase destroyed the feruloyl-CoA precursor in the test tube and hid any gingerol-forming activity [131]. Gene-expression surveys narrowed the candidates to type III polyketide synthases enriched in the rhizome [132]. In 2018 a transcriptome study, stating that the pathway had still not been verified in ginger, confirmed that rhizomes are the main site of gingerol accumulation, found accumulation beginning early in rhizome development, and identified five pathway genes upregulated at that stage [133].

### The genome and the regulators, 2021–2024

The genome assemblies turned candidates into a proposed route of twelve enzyme families running from phenylalanine to gingerol [16], and identified transcription factors that may control it [1]. The 2024 assembly showed that two of them, ZoMYB106 and ZobHLH148, bind the promoters of two caffeoyl-CoA O-methyltransferase genes in the pathway and switch them on [25].

### The key enzyme, 2026

The step the 1980 work had inferred, the condensation that makes 6-dehydrogingerdione, has now been shown with ginger's own enzymes. Expressing pairs of ginger polyketide synthases in bacteria supplied with feruloyl- and hexanoyl-CoA, a 2026 study found that one, ZoPKS2, makes 6-dehydrogingerdione as its major product, while its partner ZoPKS1 is a curcumin synthase of the kind turmeric uses [2]. The authors note that genes for gingeroid biosynthesis in ginger had not previously been known [2]. The reductions that turn the dione into gingerol remain to be shown enzyme by enzyme in the sources read here.

<!-- figure: fig-pathway -->

The aromatic oil is better mapped than the pungent fraction. A single 2012 study identified 25 monoterpene and 18 sesquiterpene synthases from ginger and turmeric and characterised more than twenty of them [31], and the enzyme that turns geraniol into the citrus-scented citral has been cloned [32].

<!-- section: 13-what-the-compounds-do-for-the-plant -->
## 13. What the compounds do for the plant

The greenhouse team asked a direct question: what are these compounds for, from the plant's side? The literature answers it only indirectly, and this section is short for that reason. <!-- framing -->

### Where the compounds sit

The gingerols are concentrated in the stele and cortex of the rhizome [24], tissues riddled with oil cells and cavities that multiply as the rhizome matures [23]. Accumulation begins early in rhizome development [133]. A storage organ that is packed with reactive, bitter or pungent compounds from its early stages is the pattern ecologists associate with chemical defence, but placement is not proof of purpose. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

### Evidence of activity against microbes

Bioassay-guided fractionation of an African land race identified 6-, 8- and 10-gingerol and 6-gingerdiol as its main antifungal compounds, though the organisms tested were 13 human pathogens [134]. Ginger's essential oil fully inhibited growth and spore germination of a taro pathogen, *Phytophthora colocasiae*, at 1250 ppm in the laboratory [135]. Against ginger's own bacterial wilt pathogen, *Ralstonia solanacearum*, a ginger extract in which 393 compounds were identified was bactericidal in a dose-dependent way, damaged the pathogen's membranes and slowed the disease on ginger plants [136]. Most directly, when ginger was infected with *Ralstonia*, the composition of its root exudates changed, and the exudates that increased lowered the disease index from 77.50% to 40.00%, which the authors read as ginger secreting antibacterial compounds in its own defence [137]. None of these studies isolates the gingerols as the active defence in a living plant.

### The plant's enemies and allies

Ginger is susceptible to at least 24 pathogens, among them viruses, bacteria, fungi and nematodes, and the *Pythium* water moulds are the most damaging because they can rot the rhizome at any stage, including in storage [138]. *Pythium gracile*, the first species reported on ginger, was described in India in 1907, and 15 species have since been implicated [138]. A comparison of healthy and rotting plants concluded that the assembly of the whole root microbial community, not any single microbe, determined plant health, and linked that assembly to particular metabolites, lipids most of all [139]. Inside healthy rhizomes, four endophytic bacterial strains have been isolated, one a *Pseudomonas* that makes a plant hormone and other growth-promoting compounds [140]. A wild relative resistant to soft rot, *Zingiber zerumbet*, switches on a set of defence and signalling genes when challenged by *Pythium* [141].

What these studies establish is that ginger's tissues and exudates can suppress microbes and that an infected plant changes what it releases. What none of them shows is that the gingerols in a living rhizome protect it from the organisms that attack it. That remains a reasonable hypothesis, untested in the sources read here. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

<!-- section: 14-growing-ginger-under-glass -->
## 14. Growing ginger under glass

This section gathers what the physiological and horticultural literature says about heat, water, light and growing systems, as background for anyone growing ginger in a greenhouse. It is not a cultivation guide. <!-- framing -->

### Heat, water and light

Heat and standing water are each damaging and worse together. Holding plants during rhizome growth at day temperatures of 28, 33 or 38 °C, with or without waterlogging, showed that both stresses irreversibly reduced photosynthetic pigments and carbon assimilation, and that photosynthesis was more sensitive to the combination than to either alone [142]. Combined high temperature and intense light caused leaves to curl, yellow and wilt, with falling chlorophyll and rising markers of membrane damage [143]. Light quality matters as well as quantity: supplementing the green band of sunlight raised ginger's photosynthetic rate and the efficiency of photosystem II [144]. Day length steers the plant between leaf growth and rhizome swelling, with short days favouring the rhizome in the Japanese cultivars tested [22].

### The rhizome's development

As the rhizome enlarges, its levels of four plant hormones (zeatin, gibberellic acid, indole acetic acid and jasmonic acid) rise in step with its diameter [145]. Gingerols begin to accumulate early in that development [133], and oil cells multiply as it matures [23].

### Soil, soilless and hydroponic systems

Ginger has been grown successfully out of soil. In a Florida comparison, a greenhouse hydroponic system gave higher yields and better-quality rhizomes than field growing, with lower costs for disease, insect and weed control [146]. In a Malaysian fertigation trial of organic substrates, plants in 100% coir dust grew best and yielded most of five substrate mixes tested [147]. In the short temperate season of the US Delmarva Peninsula, tissue-culture transplants with three or more shoots gave the highest rhizome yields, up to 648.3 g per plant in a high tunnel in the first year [148]. None of the sources read compares the gingerol content of hydroponic and soil-grown ginger directly, which is the comparison the greenhouse team is running.

### Disease under cover

Protected growing does not keep disease out. Ginger wilt, caused by *Ralstonia pseudosolanacearum*, was first detected in Minnesota in 2023 in high-tunnel ginger; it spreads through contaminated seed rhizome, soil and water [149]. Clean planting material matters for a crop propagated from its own rhizomes, which is the reason tissue-culture propagation was developed [18].

# Part V. Joining the records

<!-- section: 15-from-rhizome-to-kitchen -->
## 15. From rhizome to kitchen

What a preparation delivers depends on what was done to the rhizome before anyone ate it. This is where the chemistry of section 3 meets the recipes and the trials. <!-- framing -->

### Heat, water and time

Fresh rhizome extracted at room temperature contains gingerols and no detectable shogaols [17]; shogaol and zingerone are products of heat or alkali, not natural constituents [3]. In water, 6-gingerol and 6-shogaol interconvert reversibly; at 100 °C and pH 1 the reaction reached equilibrium within two hours, and the compounds were most stable at pH 4 [30]. Moist heat converts gingerols to shogaols more efficiently than dry heat: moist heat at 120 °C for six hours gave the highest yield in one study, 2991 mg of 6-shogaol per kilogram of ginger [150]. Hot-air drying at 120 to 180 °C likewise lowered gingerols and raised shogaols in step with temperature and time [151]. Gentler sun-drying keeps more of the volatile oil [151].

<!-- figure: fig-drying -->

### What this means for a cup of tea

No study read for this review measures what a household infusion of fresh ginger extracts. Two findings bear on it. Commercial ginger teas, supplements and beverages vary widely in their gingerol and shogaol content [37], and a mild acidity is where 6-gingerol is most stable [30]. A tea of fresh rhizome steeped briefly in hot water should therefore be a gingerol drink with little shogaol, while long boiling, drying the rhizome first or adding acid would shift the balance; that is an inference from the chemistry, not a measurement. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

### The enzyme

Fresh ginger also carries zingibain, a protease that digests collagen readily, works best near 60 °C and is destroyed at about 70 °C [35]. The same activity clots milk [36]. Fresh ginger in a marinade, or juice stirred into warm milk, is doing enzymatic work that dried ginger or a boiling pot cannot. <!-- synthesis -->

### Reading the old recipes through the chemistry

Historical recipes specify the form of ginger more often than one might expect, and the form matters. The fourteenth-century English ginger sauce calls for powdered ginger stirred cold into vinegar [152]: dried material, and so on the chemical evidence a source of shogaols as well as gingerols. Medieval hippocras was a spiced wine made from a powder of cinnamon, white Mecca ginger, grains of paradise, nutmeg and galingale [49]. The ginger beers of the nineteenth century boiled bruised dried ginger with sugar and fermented it [153, 154]; the Chinese and Indian domestic remedies used the juice of the fresh rhizome [38]; and the Chinese pharmacopoeia kept fresh and dried ginger as separate drugs [4]. Seen this way, the traditional distinction between fresh and dried ginger and the modern chemistry of gingerol and shogaol describe the same fact from two directions. <!-- synthesis -->

Most clinical trials gave dried, powdered ginger in capsules, and some gave standardised extracts [5]. Neither tells us directly about the fresh-ginger tea the greenhouse serves. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

### The recipe collection

The recipes section of this guide gathers thirty-two preparations from dated sources, from Roman spiced salts [44] to a Chinese American pickle of 1914 [155]. Ginger beer travelled furthest: much the same drink of boiling water, sugar, an acid and ginger appears in Virginia in 1824 [153], in Madras in 1850, started with palm toddy in place of brewer's yeast [156], and in London in 1861 [154]. Gingerbread moved the other way: Hannah Glasse's gingerbreads of the 1740s put beaten ginger first among the spices with treacle and butter [157], Eliza Acton's of 1845 is leavened with carbonate of soda [158], and American versions became molasses cakes in which ginger is one spice among several [159, 160]. The medicinal recipes run alongside: Ayurvedic spice powders and confections built on dried ginger [45], and English herbal practice adding ginger to purges to settle the stomach [54]. <!-- synthesis -->

### A note on gingerbread

"Gingerbread" did not always contain ginger. A fifteenth-century English recipe for *gyngerbrede* is a honey-and-breadcrumb confection spiced with saffron, pepper and cinnamon, with no ginger at all [161]; an American molasses gingerbread of 1796 likewise names cinnamon and coriander or allspice and no ginger, while the same book's "soft gingerbread" uses four ounces [159]. <!-- framing -->

<!-- section: 16-methods-of-the-field -->
## 16. Methods of the field

Each record has characteristic weaknesses, and knowing them is most of what is needed to read this literature well. <!-- framing -->

### Trials and reviews

Clinical evidence on ginger is abundant and uneven. Of 109 randomised trials reviewed in 2020, 39.4% were judged to provide high-quality evidence [91]. Reviews of reviews now exist in number: one counted 27 systematic reviews in 2019 and found their quality heterogeneous and only one reporting the funding of its included studies [162]; another, in 2022, covered 24 reviews with only 3% overlap of primary studies [5]; a third found 49% overlap among seven meta-analyses of pregnancy nausea [78]. A large count of meta-analyses is not a large count of independent experiments. The test article is the weakest point: products vary several-fold in their gingerol content [37], and the umbrella review notes poor reporting of what was given [5].

### Laboratory and animal work

Mechanistic work uses pure compounds at set concentrations in cells and isolated tissue [117, 118], while human pharmacokinetics finds the compounds circulating as conjugates and metabolites [8, 125]. An effect at a concentration that is never reached in blood is a lead, not an explanation.

### Plant science

Function is usually inferred from gene expression and from laboratory inhibition tests [16, 134]. The 2026 demonstration of a gingerol-pathway enzyme in bacteria [2] shows the standard of evidence that pathway claims can now meet.

### The historical record

The documentary record is selective: it preserves what was written down by physicians, merchants and cooks, mostly in Europe, China and India. Secondary statements about ginger's history in biomedical papers often repeat one another without a primary source, as with its origin (section 4). Nineteenth-century scholarly compilations such as *Pharmacographia* [11] remain the best guides to the primary texts and are cited here for that reason.

<!-- section: 17-open-questions -->
## 17. Open questions

These are the points on which the sources read disagree or fall silent. <!-- framing -->

- **Vomiting in pregnancy.** Single trials found both nausea and vomiting reduced [73]; pooled analyses find only nausea [77].
- **Chemotherapy.** Positive [85, 86], negative [87] and mixed [88, 90] results remain unreconciled.
- **Motion sickness.** A positive first study [64] and a positive sea trial [66] against several negative laboratory studies [67, 68], yet accepted by the European regulator [7].
- **Lipids.** Two meta-analyses of similar size disagree [101, 102].
- **Safety in pregnancy.** No harm detected in a cohort of more than a thousand exposed women [6]; regulators still advise avoidance as a precaution [7].
- **Anticoagulants.** Reassuring small studies [110, 111] against one signal in patients [112].
- **The active molecule.** Free gingerols are not found in blood after oral ginger [8].
- **The rest of the pathway.** The key synthase is now known [2]; the reductions that complete gingerol are not yet shown.
- **What gingerols do for the plant.** Untested directly [134, 137].
- **Where ginger was domesticated.** Asserted in several forms, demonstrated in none [1, 27].

<!-- section: 18-how-to-read-this-literature -->
## 18. How to read this literature

A few habits will save a reader of the ginger literature a good deal of time. <!-- framing -->

Ask first what was tested: fresh rhizome, dried powder, a two-species extract [93] or a standardised one [88] are different materials, and fresh and dried ginger differ chemically [17]. Ask what was measured: severity or incidence, nausea or vomiting, a symptom or a laboratory value. Prefer the umbrella reviews and the regulator's monograph for orientation [5, 7], and go to the individual trials before repeating a number. Treat any statement about ginger's history in a biomedical paper as a lead to be checked against the primary record. And where a cell-culture result is offered as an explanation of a clinical one, recall that the compound may not reach the tissue in that form [8]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->
