# Abstract

Ginger (*Zingiber officinale*) is read here through three records at once: the documents that describe its use, the human trials that test it, and the biology of the plant. The plant is a sterile, clonally propagated cultigen whose genome was assembled only in 2021 [1, 2, 3]. Its pungent gingerols convert to shogaols on drying and heating, so fresh and dried ginger are different materials [3, 4]. In people, the best-supported effect is on nausea in pregnancy; evidence for other uses is positive in direction and low in certainty, and reports of harm are few but the trials were small [5, 6]. After oral ginger the free compounds are not detected in blood, which complicates every proposed mechanism [7]. What the compounds do for the plant has not been tested directly in the sources read [8, 9]. The historical record consulted is partial: only openly readable sources were used. This review is a snapshot as of 24 November 2026, drafted from abstracts and record pages, not full texts.

<!-- section: part-i -->
# Part I. Foundations

<!-- section: 1-why-three-records -->
## 1. Why ginger, and why three records at once

Ginger is unusual in being at once an ancient trade good, a kitchen staple, a regulated herbal medicine and a sequenced crop. Each of those identities has its own literature, and they seldom cite one another. A historian can say what a fourteenth-century cook did with powdered ginger, a trialist whether a capsule eased nausea, and a plant biologist which genes are active in a swelling rhizome. None of the three can say alone whether the cook's dish contained what the trialist tested. This review sets the records side by side and marks, section by section, where one supports, complicates or contradicts another. It reports what its sources say and where they stop. <!-- framing -->

<!-- section: 2-the-plant-in-brief -->
## 2. The plant in brief

The rest of this review depends on three facts about the plant: where it sits among its relatives, that it is propagated without seed, and how large and unusual its genome is. <!-- framing -->

Ginger belongs to the Zingiberaceae, a pantropical family and the largest in the order Zingiberales, with 53 genera and over 1200 species [10]. The family's classification was rebuilt in 2002 from DNA sequence data, which showed that several of the morphological traits used since 1889 had arisen more than once and that three of the four traditional tribes were not natural groups; the replacement recognises four subfamilies [10]. Within the family, the genus *Zingiber* holds about 100–150 species spread across tropical and subtropical Asia and Far East Asia, many of them valued as crops, medicines or ornamentals [11]. *Zingiber officinale* is the type species of the family [1].

Ginger is a monocotyledonous, sterile cultigen [3]. It is grown in tropical and subtropical regions [12], and the part that is harvested and studied is the rhizome [3, 12].

Three genome assemblies now exist, each from a different Chinese cultivar and each resolved into two haplotypes. The first two were published together in 2021: one reports haplotypes of 1.53 Gb and 1.51 Gb [1], the other a diploid assembly anchored to 11 pseudochromosome pairs with a total length of 3.1 Gb [2]. A third, from a northern Chinese cultivar, reports haplotypes of 1.55 Gb and 1.44 Gb [12].

The second assembly found marked structural variation between the two haplotypes, and its authors suggest that two inversions larger than 15 Mb on chromosome 4 may be associated with ginger's infertility [2]. That is offered as a possibility, not a demonstrated cause.

Taken together, these sources describe a plant that people maintain entirely by planting pieces of rhizome, and whose sterility may now have a candidate structural explanation in its genome. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

<!-- section: 3-the-chemistry-in-brief -->
## 3. The chemistry in brief

Two groups of compounds carry most of what people notice about ginger: the pungent phenolics and the aromatic volatile oil. Later sections return to each; this one fixes the vocabulary. <!-- framing -->

The standard reference for ginger's flavour chemistry is a two-part 1983 monograph that reviews the components responsible for the aroma and the pungency that characterise the spice, alongside its production, processing and quality evaluation [13, 14].

The pungency of fresh ginger is due to a series of homologous phenolic ketones, the gingerols, of which 6-gingerol is the major one; 8- and 10-gingerol occur at lower concentrations [3]. A recent review groups the wider family, defined by a shared 3-methoxy-4-hydroxyphenyl unit, into gingerols, shogaols, paradols, zingerone, gingerdiones and gingerdiols, and attributes their pungency to activation of the TRPV1 receptor [15].

Gingerols are thermally unstable and can be converted to the corresponding shogaols, which are present in dried ginger [3]. In a survey of fresh rhizomes from 17 Australian clones, shogaols were not identified in extracts prepared at ambient temperature, which the authors take as confirmation that shogaols are not native constituents of fresh ginger [3]. Drying studies point the same way. Hot-air drying at 120, 150 and 180 °C lowered the content of 6-, 8- and 10-gingerol and raised the content of the matching shogaols [4], and an earlier comparison of fresh and dried Australian ginger found reduced gingerol content after drying [16].

<!-- figure: fig-drying -->

The aroma comes from the volatile oil, which contains a large variety of terpenoids [17]. A single enzyme, α-zingiberene/β-sesquiphellandrene synthase, is responsible for forming the major sesquiterpenoids of ginger and turmeric rhizomes [17]. Drying changes this fraction too: terpene hydrocarbons increase and some monoterpene alcohols are converted to their acetates [16].

Cultivars differ. Among the 17 Australian clones, one known as "Jamaican" had the highest concentrations of all three gingerols and was the most pungent, while experimental tetraploid clones did not differ significantly in gingerol concentration from their diploid parent [3].

The practical consequence, developed in section 15, is that fresh ginger and dried ginger are chemically different materials, so a source that specifies one cannot be assumed to describe the other. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

<!-- section: part-ii -->
# Part II. History and lore

<!-- section: 4-origin-and-dispersal -->
## 4. Origin and dispersal

Ginger's origin cannot be established the usual way, by finding its wild populations, because none is known. What follows is therefore an account of how the question is argued, not an answer to it. <!-- framing -->

Ginger is unknown in the wild and has been propagated asexually from rhizomes for millennia, so that the sites of its original domestication and the distribution of its wild relatives have been obscured by the movements of peoples [18]. The chemical literature describes it in the same terms, as a sterile cultigen [3]. One of the 2021 genome papers states the account now most often repeated: that ginger was first cultivated and used by Austronesian people more than 3000 years ago and was later introduced to South India following the Austronesian expansion [2]. That statement appears in the paper's introduction as background; the searches for this review found no primary study that tests it. <!-- synthesis -->

The genome may eventually bear on the question from another side. The structural rearrangements proposed as a cause of ginger's infertility would, if confirmed, help explain why a plant so widely grown has left no seed-propagated lineages to compare [2]. <!-- synthesis -->

<!-- section: 5-the-old-world-record -->
## 5. The old-world record

By the early centuries of the common era ginger was already a traded and processed spice, and it was already being written into medical systems. This section rests on few sources, and its gaps are named at the end. <!-- framing -->

The earliest physical evidence read for this review comes from Oc Eo in southern Vietnam, a trading centre of the state of Funan occupied in the early centuries CE. Plant microremains recovered from the surfaces of grinding stones there were identified as ginger, together with turmeric, fingerroot, sand ginger, galangal, clove, nutmeg and cinnamon; the authors suggest that South Asian migrants or visitors brought this way of cooking to Southeast Asia about 2000 years ago [19].

In the Greek tradition, ginger is among the plants of Dioscorides' *De materia medica*; a mid-tenth-century copy made in Constantinople, now in the Morgan Library, carries an illustration of it labelled *zengiberis* [20].

Chinese medicine did not treat ginger as one drug. A recent review of the tradition sets out distinct preparations from the same rhizome (fresh ginger, dried ginger, baked or roasted ginger, and ginger charcoal), each credited with different strengths: fresh ginger with easing vomiting and inducing sweating, dried ginger with warming, the carbonised forms with stopping bleeding. The same review notes that the main chemical constituents of these preparations are nearly identical [21]. Section 15 returns to that tension.

Three bodies of evidence that belong in this section were not consulted, because no openly readable scholarly edition or study was found in the time available: the text of Dioscorides and Galen on ginger, the Indian medical literature, and the Arabic and Persian pharmacological writers. The section should be read as incomplete on exactly those points. <!-- framing -->

<!-- section: 6-europe-to-the-americas -->
## 6. Europe to the Americas

In medieval Europe ginger was a medicine because of what it was thought to be, not because of anything observed about it. <!-- framing -->

A study of three medieval pharmacology texts explains the logic. Under the humoral theory, which came from Hippocrates and Galen and reached Europe modified by Arab physicians, health was a balance of four humours, each hot or cold and wet or dry; Asian spices such as pepper, ginger and cinnamon, classed as hot and dry, were used to balance a cold and wet European diet and to treat diseases attributed to coldness and humidity [22]. The same study observes that imported spices were held to be more valuable and effective than cheap local remedies, and that Europeans mystified them [22]. A medieval Irish materia medica shows the system at work on ginger specifically: it grades the spice by degrees of heat and moisture and recommends it, among other uses, boiled in wine or ale for pain of the stomach [23].

The kitchen and the pharmacy shared the ingredient. Ginger was an essential ingredient in medieval medicine and cooking; by the late 1500s it was used less in savoury dishes and remained popular in sweet ones [24]. It reached Europe in several forms, among them preserved in sugar syrup and dried and powdered [24]. *The Forme of Cury*, compiled about 1390 by the master cooks of Richard II, uses it throughout [25]; a nineteenth-century editor of later English cookery manuscripts lists ginger among the seasonings that "appear constantly in dishes where we should little expect them" [26].

Ginger then crossed the Atlantic. Edible varieties were transplanted to the Caribbean by Spanish colonists in the 1500s [24], and "Jamaican" survives as the name of a cultivar, which in one modern survey was the most pungent of seventeen clones tested [3].

The darkest episode in ginger's American history was not caused by ginger. In 1930 an illicit extract of Jamaica ginger, sold to get around the Prohibition laws, had been adulterated with triorthocresyl phosphate, a neurotoxic organophosphate, and thousands of Americans were poisoned [27]. A later account puts the number ultimately affected by partial paralysis at 50,000 [28]. Survivors examined forty-seven years on still showed the spasticity and abnormal reflexes of spinal cord damage [27].

<!-- section: 7-why-people-spice-food -->
## 7. Why people spice food

One influential answer to why cuisines use spices at all comes from evolutionary biology, and ginger is part of the dataset behind it. <!-- framing -->

The antimicrobial hypothesis holds that spices are used because they inhibit or kill food-spoilage microorganisms. Its authors tabulated 43 spices in 4578 meat-based recipes from 93 traditional cookbooks covering 36 countries, and found that as mean annual temperature rose, so did the proportion of recipes containing spices, the number of spices per recipe and the use of the most potent antibacterial spices [29]. They considered four alternatives (that spices supply nutrients, disguise spoilage, promote cooling by perspiration, or do nothing useful) and found none well supported by their data [29, 30].

The evidence is a correlation across cookbooks, and the published responses to it were not read for this review. Two points from elsewhere in this review bear on it. Ginger's compounds do inhibit microbes in the laboratory (section 13), which fits the hypothesis; and the medieval European sources of section 6 give a different stated reason for spicing food, the balancing of humours, which is a reminder that the reasons people give and the reasons a practice persists need not be the same. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

<!-- section: part-iii -->
# Part III. Health: the human evidence

<!-- section: 8-nausea-and-vomiting -->
## 8. Nausea and vomiting

Nausea is the use for which ginger has been tested most often, and the answer differs by setting. This section takes pregnancy, chemotherapy, surgery and motion in turn, and keeps nausea and vomiting apart, because the trials often move one and not the other. Where a dose appears, it is what that study gave its participants. <!-- framing -->

The weight of the literature is itself a finding. In a 2019 overview of 27 systematic reviews of ginger for any condition, nausea and vomiting was the subject of 12 of them [31]. The 2022 umbrella review, covering 24 systematic reviews, placed the antiemetic effect in pregnant women at the top of its ranking, with a large effect size and a high GRADE rating, and rated postoperative nausea and chemotherapy-induced vomiting lower, at low to moderate certainty [5].

**Pregnancy.** An early randomised, double-masked trial assigned 70 women at or before 17 weeks' gestation to 1 g of oral ginger a day or placebo for four days; nausea scores and the number of vomiting episodes both fell significantly more with ginger, and 28 of 32 women in the ginger group reported improvement against 10 of 35 on placebo [32]. A 2014 meta-analysis of twelve trials involving 1278 women found that ginger significantly improved nausea compared with placebo but did not significantly reduce the number of vomiting episodes, though there was a trend in that direction [6]. A 2024 umbrella review of seven meta-analyses, drawing on 22 independent studies, reached the same conclusion for nausea, and rated the quality of those meta-analyses as critically low to low [33]. The single trial and the pooled analyses therefore agree on nausea and disagree on vomiting.

**Chemotherapy.** Here the trials conflict. The largest, a double-blind multicentre study that analysed 576 patients all receiving a standard antiemetic, found that every ginger dose tested reduced the severity of acute nausea on the first day of chemotherapy compared with placebo [34]. A 2022 systematic review of 23 trials found no significant difference between ginger and control in the incidence of acute nausea, delayed nausea, acute vomiting or delayed vomiting overall, and a significant reduction only in acute vomiting within one dosing subgroup [35]. A 2023 placebo-controlled trial in 103 adults, using a standardised extract, then reported the opposite pattern: benefit for nausea-related quality of life and for delayed nausea and vomiting, and no effect on anticipatory or acute symptoms [36]. One trial measured severity, the review counted incidence, and the later trial used a standardised product, so the three results are not straightforwardly comparable.

**Surgery.** A 2006 meta-analysis pooled five randomised trials with 363 patients and reported summary relative risks of 0.69 for postoperative nausea and vomiting and 0.61 for postoperative vomiting, favouring ginger over placebo [37].

**Motion.** The experimental evidence read so far is one crossover study in thirteen volunteers with a history of motion sickness, in which ginger pretreatment reduced nausea, abnormal gastric electrical activity and the rise in plasma vasopressin provoked by a rotating visual field [38]. European regulators nonetheless accept powdered ginger rhizome for preventing nausea and vomiting in motion sickness on the basis of clinical studies, which is its one "well-established" use in the European Union monograph [39]. The assessment has not always been favourable: a 2005 review concluded that clinical evidence beyond doubt existed only for pregnancy-related nausea and vomiting, and that meta-analyses had not demonstrated an effect after surgery or in motion sickness [40].

Across settings the quality of the underlying trials is a recurring caveat. A systematic review of 109 randomised trials judged only 43 of them (39.4%) to provide high-quality evidence and named small study populations and unstandardised outcome measures as the common weaknesses [41].

<!-- section: 9-pain-inflammation-and-metabolic-outcomes -->
## 9. Pain, inflammation and metabolic outcomes

Beyond nausea, ginger has been tested mainly for pain and for markers of metabolic and inflammatory status. The pattern in this section is consistent: pooled effects are usually in ginger's favour, and the certainty attached to them is usually low. <!-- framing -->

**Osteoarthritis.** The umbrella review rates ginger's analgesic effect in osteoarthritis as small, with high certainty [5]. The trial most often cited behind it enrolled 261 patients with knee osteoarthritis for six weeks: 63% of the extract group and 50% of the placebo group met the response criterion for knee pain on standing, an effect its authors call moderate [42]. Two details of that trial matter for anyone reading it as evidence about ginger. The product was a concentrated extract of two species, *Zingiber officinale* and *Alpinia galanga*, and gastrointestinal adverse events, mostly mild, were reported by 59 patients on the extract against 21 on placebo [42]. A 2005 review had judged that the usefulness of proprietary ginger preparations for osteoarthritic pain remained to be confirmed [40].

**Period pain.** A 2015 meta-analysis of four placebo-controlled trials found a significant reduction in pain scores with ginger taken during the first three to four days of the cycle [43]. A 2024 meta-analysis with twelve studies in its quantitative synthesis found ginger more effective than placebo for pain intensity (standardised mean difference −1.13, with heterogeneity of 81.05%) and no different from non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs; its authors note that safety data were infrequently reported and that risk of bias limits the finding [44].

**Migraine.** One double-blind trial randomised sixty adults attending an emergency room with a migraine attack to ginger extract or placebo on top of intravenous ketoprofen, and found a significantly better clinical response with ginger at one, one and a half and two hours [45]. It is a single trial of an add-on.

**Blood sugar and lipids.** Two meta-analyses in type 2 diabetes, each of ten studies, report lower fasting glucose and lower glycated haemoglobin with ginger: a pooled difference in HbA1c of −1.00 in the first [46] and −0.57 in the second, where heterogeneity was 88.6% [47]. They disagree about lipids. The first reports significant improvement in lipid profile [46]; the second found no significant influence on triglycerides, total, LDL or HDL cholesterol, while reporting small reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure [47]. The umbrella review's grading reflects that instability: an effect on glycaemic control ranging from none to very large at very low to moderate certainty, and a small effect on lipids at very low certainty [5].

**Inflammatory markers.** A 2025 dose–response meta-analysis of 29 trials reports reductions in C-reactive protein (weighted mean difference −0.86 mg/L), tumour necrosis factor α and interleukin-6, and cautions that high heterogeneity calls for careful interpretation [48]. A 2026 umbrella review of 36 publications reaches a favourable overall conclusion on inflammation, glycaemic and lipid measures [49]. A 2025 review of meta-analyses agrees on direction and points to heterogeneity of 98.1% in one of them, and to possible bias in blinding in the trials behind another [50].

Most of what is pooled in this section is a laboratory value or a pain score over a few weeks. None of the sources read here reports an effect of ginger on a disease outcome such as a diabetic complication or a cardiovascular event. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

<!-- section: 10-safety-interactions-and-pregnancy -->
## 10. Safety, interactions and pregnancy

Ginger is a food, and the evidence on harm is correspondingly thin: trials were designed to detect benefit, and most were small and short. This section reports what has been looked for and what has not been found, which is different from what has been ruled out. <!-- framing -->

The umbrella review concludes that dietary consumption of ginger appears safe [5]. The adverse effects that trials do record are mainly gastrointestinal: mostly mild events in the osteoarthritis trial described above [42], and belching identified as a potential side effect in pregnant women [50]. An older comprehensive review is more guarded, stating that preclinical safety data do not rule out potential toxicity and that longer-term consumption in particular should be monitored [40].

**Pregnancy.** The trials have not detected harm. The 70-woman trial found no adverse effect on pregnancy outcome [32], and the 2024 umbrella review found no evidence of significant adverse effects [33]. The 2014 meta-analysis found no significant increase in spontaneous abortion compared with placebo, with a relative risk of 3.14 and a 95% confidence interval from 0.65 to 15.11 [6]. An interval that wide is compatible with no effect and with a substantial one, so this is an absence of evidence of harm in a small sample. <!-- synthesis --> European regulators take the cautious side: the public summary of the European Union monograph names pregnancy and breast-feeding among its cautions for ginger medicines, and children under six for the motion-sickness use because adequate data are lacking [39].

**Blood thinning.** The concern comes from the laboratory. In human whole blood in vitro, 8-gingerol, 8-shogaol and 8-paradol inhibited platelet activation at concentrations of 3 to 7 micromolar, more potently than aspirin under the same conditions [51]. The human evidence does not bear the concern out so far. In a crossover study of twelve healthy men, ginger at recommended doses affected neither clotting status nor the pharmacokinetics or pharmacodynamics of warfarin [52]. A review of the published record to 1999 found evidence lacking for an interaction between warfarin and ginger [53]. A 2019 overview could not draw a conclusion on ginger and platelet aggregation [31], and the European summary states that no interactions with other medicines had been described in the literature at the time of assessment [39]. Twelve healthy volunteers are not patients on long-term anticoagulation, and a confirmatory study was called for as long ago as 2005 [40].

<!-- section: 11-how-it-might-work-and-the-bioavailability-problem -->
## 11. How it might work, and the bioavailability problem

Several mechanisms have been proposed for ginger's effect on nausea, most of them demonstrated in cells or isolated tissue. Whether they operate in a person depends on how much of which compound reaches which tissue, and that is where the account becomes difficult. <!-- framing -->

**What reaches the blood.** The first human pharmacokinetic study gave 27 volunteers single oral doses from 100 mg to 2.0 g. No participant had detectable free 6-gingerol, 8-gingerol, 10-gingerol or 6-shogaol in plasma; the compounds appeared as glucuronide and, less often, sulfate conjugates, peaked at about an hour and had elimination half-lives under two hours [7]. A later study in asthma patients taking ginger extract for 56 days found that about one-third of 6-gingerol was converted to gingerdiols and more than 90% of 6-shogaol to other metabolites, with all compounds peaking in under two hours [54]. Laboratory and mouse work points to the cause: ginger phenolics were rapidly cleared by liver microsomes from four species including humans, and the authors conclude that first-pass metabolism, particularly glucuronide conjugation, underlies the low systemic exposure [55].

<!-- figure: fig-cmax -->

**Serotonin receptors.** The best-supported mechanism for the antiemetic effect is inhibition of the 5-HT3 receptor, the target of standard antiemetic drugs. Ginger extracts, 6-gingerol and 6-shogaol inhibited activation of human 5-HT3 receptors expressed in cells and of native receptors in preparations of human gut nerve tissue, at low micromolar concentrations and in a non-competitive manner [56]. In guinea-pig tissue the effect on 5-HT3 and on muscarinic M3 receptors was described as weak, and 5-HT4 receptors appeared not to be involved [57]. A review of the chemotherapy context lists further candidates, among them substance P and acetylcholine receptor antagonism and effects on gastric emptying and vasopressin release [58].

**The stomach.** Two small human studies measured gastric function directly. In 24 healthy volunteers the gastric half-emptying time was 13.1 minutes after ginger and 26.7 minutes after placebo [59]; in 11 patients with functional dyspepsia it was 12.3 against 16.1 minutes [60]. Neither study found a difference in symptoms [59, 60]. In the motion study of section 8, ginger reduced abnormal gastric rhythm and vasopressin release along with nausea [38].

<!-- figure: fig-gastric -->

**Pungency.** Gingerols, shogaols and zingerone activate TRPV1, the receptor that also responds to capsaicin, and depend on the same two residues in the channel that capsaicin does [61]. In rat cells the shogaols were more potent than the gingerols, and 10-shogaol activated the receptor without being pungent [62].

The receptor studies work at micromolar concentrations of the free compounds, while the human studies find conjugates and metabolites, not the free compounds, in blood. One reading is that ginger acts mainly on the gut wall, which is exposed before first-pass metabolism; another is that the metabolites are themselves active, which the asthma study's authors raise [54]. The sources read here do not settle it. <!-- synthesis -->

# Part IV. The plant itself

<!-- section: 12-the-genome-and-the-making-of-gingerols -->
## 12. The genome and the making of gingerols

How ginger builds its pungent compounds has been inferred in stages over twenty years, and the inference is still ahead of the proof. This section separates what has been proposed from what has been shown. <!-- framing -->

The starting point is that the gingerols of ginger and the curcuminoids of turmeric accumulate at high levels in the rhizome and appear to derive from the phenylpropanoid pathway [63]. In 2006, crude protein extracts from ginger and turmeric leaf, shoot and rhizome were shown to carry the activities of the phenylpropanoid enzymes tested [63]. The decisive step could not be observed in ginger, however: a thioesterase activity in ginger extracts hydrolysed the feruloyl-CoA precursor, which prevented a gingerol synthase activity from being identified [63]. The authors suggested that such activities may divert intermediates away from gingerol production and so play a regulatory role [63].

Gene-expression surveys then narrowed the candidates. A collection of expressed sequence tags from eight ginger and turmeric tissues found rhizome-enriched transcripts associated with the phenylpropanoid pathway and with putative type III polyketide synthases, which the authors read as reinforcing earlier predictions that this enzyme class makes curcuminoids and gingerols [64]. Their pathway diagram is explicit about its limits: every conversion had been demonstrated in other species except those catalysed by the polyketide synthases, the reductases and some turmeric-specific steps [64]. A 2018 transcriptome study, which states that the gingerol pathway had not been verified in ginger, confirmed that rhizomes are the major source of gingerols, found that accumulation begins at an early stage of rhizome development, and identified five pathway-associated genes that were upregulated at that stage [65].

The genome assemblies turned candidates into a proposed route. One 2021 assembly, combining genome, transcriptome and metabolome data, proposes a backbone pathway for gingerol analogues built from 12 enzyme gene families (PAL, C4H, 4CL, CST, C3'H, C3OMT, CCOMT, CSE, PKS, AOR, DHN and DHT) together with the transcription-factor networks likely to regulate it [1]. Its companion paper predicts transcription factors that may regulate 6-gingerol biosynthesis [2].

The 2024 assembly goes a step further toward mechanism. It reports a lineage-specific expansion of genes involved in stilbenoid, diarylheptanoid and gingerol biosynthesis, and shows by yeast one-hybrid, electrophoretic mobility shift and dual-luciferase assays that two transcription factors, ZoMYB106 and ZobHLH148, bind the promoters of ZoCCOMT1 and ZoCCOMT2 and promote their transcription [12].

The aromatic fraction is better characterised at the enzyme level than the pungent one. Before 2012 only four terpene synthases were known from the whole family; a single study then identified 25 monoterpene and 18 sesquiterpene synthases from ginger and turmeric and functionally characterised 13 and 11 of them [17].

Read together, the sources consulted here describe a pathway that is proposed from expression and genomic evidence and supported at the level of gene regulation, while none of them reports an isolated ginger enzyme shown to make a gingerol. That gap may have been closed in work this review has not yet read. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

<!-- section: 13-what-the-compounds-do-for-the-plant -->
## 13. What the compounds do for the plant

The greenhouse team asked a direct question: what are these compounds for, from the plant's side? The literature found so far answers it only indirectly, and this section is short for that reason. <!-- framing -->

The evidence closest to the question concerns bacterial wilt, described as one of the most important diseases of ginger [9]. When ginger was infected with *Ralstonia solanacearum*, the composition of its root exudates changed, and the exudates that increased inhibited the disease, lowering the disease index from 77.50% to 40.00%; the authors conclude that ginger secretes antibacterial compounds in its own defence [9]. Separately, a ginger extract in which 393 compounds were identified killed the same pathogen in a dose-dependent manner, with a minimum inhibitory concentration of 3.91 mg/ml and a minimum bactericidal concentration of 125 mg/ml, damaged its cell membrane, and delayed or slowed wilt on ginger plants [66]. Neither study attributes the effect to gingerols specifically.

For the gingerols themselves, the antimicrobial evidence comes from outside the plant's own ecology. Bioassay-guided fractionation of an African land race identified 6-, 8- and 10-gingerol and 6-gingerdiol as the main antifungal principles, but the organisms tested were 13 human pathogens [8]. Ginger essential oil fully inhibited growth and spore germination of *Phytophthora colocasiae* at 1250 ppm in the laboratory, but that is a pathogen of taro, and the oil tested was 89.05% citral [67].

Ginger's own enemies are well catalogued. Pythium soft rot was first reported on ginger in India in 1907, and 15 *Pythium* species have since been implicated [68]. Rhizome rot has been attributed to several different individual causal agents [69].

A newer line of work shifts attention from single pathogens to the community around the plant. A comparison of healthy and diseased ginger concluded that the assembly of the microbiome, not the presence of any one microbe, determined plant health, and linked that assembly to specific metabolites, with lipids and lipid-like molecules the largest group associated with health [69]. Inside the rhizome, four endophytic bacterial strains have been isolated, one of them a *Pseudomonas* able to produce a plant hormone, ACC deaminase and a siderophore; the same paper notes that the functional role of the rhizome's microorganisms was at the time unexplored [70].

What these studies establish is that ginger tissue and exudates can suppress microbes, and that an infected plant changes what it releases. What none of them shows is that gingerols in a living rhizome protect it from the organisms that actually attack it. The defensive role of ginger's pungent compounds is a reasonable hypothesis that, in the sources read here, has not been tested directly. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

<!-- section: 14-growing-ginger-under-glass -->
## 14. Growing ginger under glass

This section gathers what the physiological literature says about heat, water and light, as background for anyone growing the plant in a greenhouse. It is not a cultivation guide, and the studies behind it were done on field crops in China. <!-- framing -->

Heat and standing water are each damaging and worse together. During the rhizome growth period, plants were held at day/night temperatures of 28/22 °C, 33/27 °C or 38/32 °C, with or without waterlogging [71]. Waterlogging and high temperature irreversibly decreased photosynthetic pigment content, increased reactive oxygen species in the leaves, inhibited carbon assimilation and limited electron transport in photosystem II, and photosynthesis was more sensitive to the combined stress than to either alone [71].

Strong light compounds the problem. A 2023 study of combined high-temperature and intense-light stress describes ginger as vulnerable to that combination and, summarising earlier work, characterises it as a shade-loving species with a suitable daily average temperature of 20–28 °C [72].

Light quality matters as well as quantity. Supplementing the green band of sunlight raised ginger's net photosynthetic rate and both the maximal and the actual photochemical efficiency of photosystem II, compared with sunlight alone [73].

The rhizome's own growth has been followed through three stages of enlargement. The levels of zeatin, gibberellic acid, indole acetic acid and jasmonic acid rose as the rhizome enlarged, and the rise was positively correlated with rhizome diameter [74]. Gingerol accumulation, as noted in section 12, starts early in rhizome development [65].

Protected growing does not keep disease out. Ginger wilt was first detected in Minnesota in 2023, in ginger grown in high tunnels; the state's fact sheet says it can spread through contaminated seed, soil or water and advises buying seed rhizome from a reputable source [75].

The searches behind this review returned no study comparing hydroponic with soil-grown ginger among their most cited results, which is the comparison the greenhouse team is running. That absence reflects the limits of the search and should not be read as a statement that no such work exists. <!-- framing -->

<!-- section: part-v -->
# Part V. Joining the records

<!-- section: 15-from-rhizome-to-kitchen -->
## 15. From rhizome to kitchen

What a preparation delivers depends on what was done to the rhizome before it was eaten. This is where the chemistry of section 3 meets the documents of Part II and the trials of Part III. <!-- framing -->

Fresh rhizome extracted at ambient temperature contains gingerols and no detectable shogaols [3]. Drying lowers gingerol content and changes the volatile fraction [16], and hot-air drying at 120 to 180 °C converts gingerols to shogaols in step with temperature and time [4]. Steaming has its own signature: 10-shogaol, described as a component of steamed ginger, activates the TRPV1 receptor without tasting pungent [62].

Ginger also carries an enzyme that the pungency literature ignores. Zingibain, a protease first described in 1973, digests collagen far more readily than the muscle protein actomyosin and makes meat more tender; it works best at about 60 °C and is rapidly denatured at 70 °C [76]. Fresh ginger in a marinade and ginger added to a boiling pot are therefore doing different things.

Seen this way, the Chinese practice of treating fresh, dried, baked and carbonised ginger as different medicines [21] and the chemists' finding that heat and drying change what the rhizome contains [3, 4] describe the same fact from two directions, even though the review of that tradition regards the preparations' main constituents as nearly identical [21]. <!-- synthesis -->

The same applies to reading a recipe. The medieval sauce reproduced in the recipes section calls for powdered ginger stirred into vinegar and served uncooked [25]: dried material, and so on the chemical evidence a source of shogaols as well as gingerols. The trials of Part III mostly gave dried ginger in capsules [5]. Neither tells us directly about a tea made from fresh rhizome, which is what the greenhouse serves. <!-- synthesis -->

<!-- section: 16-methods-of-the-field -->
## 16. Methods of the field

Each record has characteristic weaknesses, and knowing them is most of what is needed to read this literature well. <!-- framing -->

Clinical evidence on ginger is abundant and uneven. Of 109 randomised trials reviewed in 2020, 39.4% were judged to provide high-quality evidence, with small samples and unstandardised outcome measures the usual faults [41]. Reviews of reviews now exist in number, and they grade the same underlying trials: one counted 27 systematic reviews in 2019 [31], another 24 with only 3% overlap of primary studies in 2022 [5], and a third found 49% overlap among seven meta-analyses of pregnancy nausea [33]. A large count of meta-analyses is therefore not a large count of independent experiments. <!-- synthesis --> The intervention itself is poorly standardised: the umbrella review notes substantial heterogeneity and poor reporting of what was actually given [5].

Mechanistic work is done in cells and isolated tissue at set concentrations of pure compounds [56, 57], while pharmacokinetic work shows that those compounds circulate as conjugates and metabolites [7, 54]. Plant studies infer function from gene expression and from laboratory inhibition tests [1, 8]. Historical claims in the scientific literature are often repeated from introduction to introduction without a primary source, as with ginger's origin [2].

<!-- section: 17-open-questions -->
## 17. Open questions

These are the points on which the sources read for this review disagree or fall silent. <!-- framing -->

- Does ginger reduce vomiting in pregnancy, or only nausea? A single trial found both [32]; pooled analyses find only the latter [6].
- Does it help in chemotherapy, and if so with acute or delayed symptoms? Three major sources give three answers [34, 35, 36].
- Does it improve blood lipids? Two meta-analyses of similar size disagree [46, 47].
- Is it safe in pregnancy? No harm has been detected, in samples too small to exclude it [6].
- Which molecule acts, and where? Free gingerols are not found in blood after oral ginger [7].
- Which enzyme makes gingerols? A pathway is proposed; no isolated enzyme was found in the sources read [1, 63].
- Do gingerols defend the living rhizome? Untested directly in the sources read [8, 9].
- Where was ginger domesticated? Asserted, not demonstrated, in the sources read [2, 18].

<!-- section: 18-how-to-read-this-literature -->
## 18. How to read this literature

A few habits will save a reader of the ginger literature a good deal of time. <!-- framing -->

Ask first what was tested. An extract of two species [42], a standardised extract [36] and powdered rhizome are different test articles, and fresh and dried ginger differ chemically [3]. Ask what was measured: severity or incidence, nausea or vomiting, a symptom or a laboratory marker. Prefer the umbrella reviews for orientation [5, 33] and go to the individual trials before repeating a number. Treat any statement about ginger's history that appears in a biomedical paper as a lead to be checked, not a finding. And where a cell-culture result is offered as an explanation of a clinical one, recall that the compound may not reach the tissue in that form [7]. <!-- synthesis -->
