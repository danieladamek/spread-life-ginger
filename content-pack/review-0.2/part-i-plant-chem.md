## 2. The plant in brief

The rest of this review depends on four facts about the plant: where it sits among its relatives, that it is propagated without seed, how its rhizome is built, and how large and unusual its genome is. <!-- framing -->

### A large tropical family

Ginger belongs to the Zingiberaceae, the largest family in the order Zingiberales, with 53 genera and over 1200 species across the tropics [@kress2002]. The family's classification was rebuilt in 2002 from DNA sequences, which showed that several of the floral and ovary traits used since 1889 had evolved more than once and that three of the four traditional tribes were not natural groups; the replacement recognises four subfamilies [@kress2002]. Within it, the genus *Zingiber* holds roughly 100 to 150 species in tropical and subtropical Asia, and comparisons of their complete chloroplast genomes now give the genus a solid backbone phylogeny [@jiang2023]. *Zingiber officinale* is the type species of the whole family [@li2021].

### A plant without seed

Cultivated ginger is described as a sterile cultigen, maintained entirely by planting pieces of rhizome [@wohlmuth2005; @ma2006]. Sterility is not absolute. In 21 germplasm collections examined in India, pollen that took up stain ranged from 5.59% to 67.73% and pollen that germinated in vitro from 2.35% to 60.31%; the two most fertile collections turned out to be tetraploids with 2n = 44, against the normal 2n = 22 [@ramakrishnan2016]. Chromosome studies in the 1960s had already found the cultivated clone carrying inversions and interchanges, the kind of structural rearrangement that disrupts pairing at meiosis [@ramachandran1969], and a 2021 genome found two inversions larger than 15 Mb on chromosome 4 that its authors propose may be associated with ginger's infertility [@cheng2021]. In a close relative that also propagates clonally, *Zingiber zerumbet*, the block has been located late in the process, at the point where the pollen tube should burst inside the ovule [@liang2023]. Flowering itself depends on day length in some cultivars, and short days favour swelling of the rhizome over growth of the shoot [@adaniya1989].

Clonal propagation has practical consequences. Every new plant carries whatever pathogens its parent piece carried, and multiplication of good stock is slow, which is why tissue-culture propagation was developed; micropropagated plants grown in a greenhouse did not differ chemically from conventionally propagated ones [@ma2006].

### The rhizome

What is sold as ginger "root" is a rhizome, a horizontal underground stem. Under light and electron microscopy it has an epidermis, a cortex and a central stele; its pericycle stays meristematic and keeps producing tissue inward [@liu2020]. Oil cells and oil cavities are scattered through it, the cavities forming by breakdown of cells, and as the rhizome matures its starch grains become fewer while oil cells and cavities multiply [@liu2020]. Imaging of intact rhizome sections by mass spectrometry finds the gingerols concentrated in the stele and cortex, while shogaols are spread more widely, including the epidermis [@torrecampo2026].

### The genome

Three genome assemblies, each from a Chinese cultivar and each resolved into two haplotypes, were published between 2021 and 2024. The first reports haplotypes of 1.53 and 1.51 gigabases with 39,217 and 38,090 protein-coding genes, and about 62% of the genome made of transposable elements [@li2021]. The second anchors a 3.1 Gb diploid assembly on 11 pseudochromosome pairs and predicts 73,006 gene models [@cheng2021]. The third reports haplotypes of 1.55 and 1.44 Gb, retrotransposon bursts within the last million years, a recent whole-genome duplication and a lineage-specific expansion of genes for gingerol and related pathways [@chen2024]. Across cultivars the variation is real but modest: plants of *Z. officinale* from different geographical origins were genetically indistinguishable by the markers used in one study, differing chemically only in the quantities of their gingerols [@jiang2005b]. Most marker studies of cultivated ginger find little diversity, as would be expected of a crop multiplied by cuttings [@kizhakkayil2011].

## 3. The chemistry in brief

Two groups of compounds carry most of what people notice about ginger: the pungent phenolics and the aromatic volatile oil. A third, a protein-digesting enzyme, matters in the kitchen. This section fixes the vocabulary; sections 12 and 15 develop it. <!-- framing -->

### The pungent principles

The pungent principle had a name long before it had a structure. Analyses of commercial gingers by the pharmacist J. C. Thresh, reported from 1879, already separated a "gingerol", a viscid, straw-coloured, odourless and extremely pungent fluid, from a volatile oil that was aromatic but not pungent, and found that fine Jamaica ginger carried only about half the oil of Cochin and African samples [@watt1893]. The structure of the pungent principles was settled in 1969. Acetone extracts of dried rhizome contain a series of gingerols with side chains of different lengths, while shogaol and zingerone, earlier described as natural constituents, were absent from the rhizome and formed only when gingerol or the oleoresin was treated with heat or alkali [@connell1969]. Later work confirmed the point for fresh ginger: in fresh rhizomes from 17 Australian clones, 6-gingerol was the major pungent compound, 8- and 10-gingerol occurred at lower levels, and shogaols were not detected in extracts made at room temperature [@wohlmuth2005]. A recent review groups the wider family, defined by a shared 3-methoxy-4-hydroxyphenyl unit, into gingerols, shogaols, paradols, zingerone, gingerdiones and gingerdiols, and attributes their pungency to activation of the TRPV1 receptor [@gao2022].

The gingerol molecule is unstable because of a β-hydroxy ketone group. In water it loses a molecule of water to become the corresponding shogaol, and the reaction is reversible: gingerol and shogaol interconvert, fastest at high temperature and low pH and slowest at pH 4 [@bhattarai2001]. That chemistry is why fresh and dried ginger differ, and section 15 follows its consequences into the kitchen.

### The aromatic oil

The aroma comes from the volatile oil, a mixture of terpenoids [@koo2012]. Its major sesquiterpenes, α-zingiberene and β-sesquiphellandrene, are made by a single enzyme that ginger shares with turmeric [@koo2012]. The citrus note of fresh ginger comes from monoterpenes, above all geraniol and its derivatives geranial, geranyl acetate and citronellol; a ginger enzyme that oxidises geraniol to citral has been cloned and characterised [@iijima2014]. Drying changes this fraction too, raising terpene hydrocarbons and converting some monoterpene alcohols to acetates [@bartley2000], and oil yield and composition differ between cultivars and drying methods [@kamal2023].

### The enzyme

Ginger rhizome also contains a protease, named zingibain in 1973, that digests collagen far more readily than muscle protein, works best around 60 °C and is rapidly inactivated at 70 °C [@thompson1973]. Partly purified, the same activity clots milk [@huang2011].

### How variable the material is

Ginger products vary widely in what they contain. Across nine ginger dietary supplements, the content of individual gingerols and of 6-shogaol varied with coefficients of variation between 45.7% and 141.7% [@schwertner2007]. Plants from different origins carry the same compounds in different amounts [@jiang2005b], and one Australian cultivar known as "Jamaican" had the highest gingerol content and was the most pungent of seventeen clones [@wohlmuth2005]. A trial that reports "1 g of ginger" without saying which ginger therefore says less than it seems to. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->
