# Part II. History and lore

<!-- section: 4-origin-and-dispersal -->
## 4. Origin and dispersal

Ginger's origin cannot be settled the usual way, by finding its wild populations, because none is known. What follows is an account of how the question has been argued over 150 years, and of where the arguments now stand. <!-- framing -->

### No wild ginger

Every authority read for this review agrees on the starting fact. The great pharmacological histories of the nineteenth century describe ginger as native to Asia, "universally cultivated, but not known in a wild state" [@flueckiger1879], and as cultivated in India from prehistoric times but not then known wild [@dymock1893]; an American dispensatory of 1898 gives its native country as unknown [@kings1898]. The botanist J. D. Hooker, quoted in 1903, put the problem more sharply: he knew of no botanist who had found it wild, and suspected that no one had looked [@yule1903]. Modern botany says the same thing in its own terms: ginger is unknown in the wild and has been propagated from rhizomes for millennia [@oxfordherbariand].

### Three accounts

Three accounts of where it was domesticated are current, and the sources read do not reconcile them. A 2011 review of ginger's genetic resources places its origin in the Indo-Malayan region and its domestication in India and China, which it treats as the centre of origin of the species [@kizhakkayil2011]. A 2021 genome paper states instead that ginger was first cultivated and used by Austronesian peoples more than 3000 years ago and reached South India with the Austronesian expansion [@cheng2021]; that sentence sits in the paper's introduction as background, and the searches for this review found no primary study that tests it. A Chinese tradition recorded in the early twentieth century reads the written character for ginger as pointing to an origin outside China, on the Mongolian borders [@stuart1911], which neither modern account supports. <!-- synthesis -->

The genetic evidence is thin because the crop is clonal. Plants of *Z. officinale* from different geographical origins were indistinguishable by the DNA markers used in one study [@jiang2005b], and marker studies in general find little diversity in the cultivated crop [@kizhakkayil2011]. What the genome may add is the history of the crop's sterility: the cultivated clone already carried chromosomal inversions and interchanges when it was examined in 1969 [@ramachandran1969], and the 2021 genome found large inversions it links to infertility [@cheng2021]. <!-- framing -->

### The names

The word has travelled further than the plant's documented history. English "ginger" comes, through medieval Latin and Greek, from Arabic *zanjabīl* [@yule1903]. Sanskrit *śṛṅgavera* is usually glossed "antlered", from the shape of the rhizome [@dymock1893], but the 1903 editors of *Hobson-Jobson* thought that meaning an "imaginary etymology" laid over a borrowed word, and proposed the Malayalam *inchi* (from which *inchi-ver*, "ginger root") as the deeper source [@yule1903]. Persian intermediaries are proposed for the Greek form [@dymock1893]. Arab geographers, misled by the sound of *zanjabīl*, sometimes placed ginger on the coast of Zinj in East Africa [@yule1903]. Sanskrit also gave ginger titles rather than names: *mahauṣadha*, "great remedy", and *viśvabheṣaja*, "universal medicine" [@dymock1893].

## 5. The old-world record

By the first centuries of the common era ginger was traded across the Indian Ocean, written into Greek, Indian and Chinese medicine, and eaten at the table of at least one famous diner. This section follows the documentary record; its gaps are named at the end. <!-- framing -->

<!-- figure: fig-timeline -->

### The archaeology

The earliest physical evidence read for this review comes from Oc Eo in southern Vietnam, a trading port of the early centuries CE, where starch and other microscopic plant remains on grinding stones of South Asian type were identified as ginger, turmeric, fingerroot, sand ginger, galangal, clove, nutmeg and cinnamon, the ingredients of a curry [@wang2023].

### Greece and Rome

The Greek physician Dioscorides, writing about AD 65, described ginger as a plant produced mostly in "Troglodytic Arabia" on the Red Sea, where the green plant was boiled and mixed into drinks and stews, and its small whitish roots were peppery to taste and smell [@yule1903]. He classed it as warming, digestive, gently laxative and good for the stomach, an ingredient in eye-salves and antidotes [@dymock1893]. A tenth-century Byzantine copy of his herbal illustrates the plant under the name *zengiberis* [@morganlibrarynd]. Pliny, a few years later, mentioned it briefly in his chapter on pepper: it grows in Arabia and "Troglodytica", is "of intense pungency", decays quickly, and sells at Rome for six denarii a pound [@pliny1855]. That both writers placed ginger in Arabia reflects the route, not the origin: the Romans received it through the Red Sea ports, and in the second century ginger was among the Indian goods on which duty was levied at Alexandria [@flueckiger1879]. Roman cookery used it in measured mixtures, among them a spiced salt credited with aiding digestion and warding off disease, and a digestive relish of cumin, ginger, rue, dates, pepper and honey taken with lettuce [@apicius1922].

### India

In Indian medicine ginger is fundamental. The *Suśruta Saṃhitā* groups it with long pepper and black pepper as *trikaṭu*, "the three pungents", credited with destroying fat and phlegm and curing catarrh, dullness of appetite and indigestion [@bhishagratna1907]. The later Sanskrit lexicons distinguish fresh ginger (*ārdraka*) from dried (*śuṇṭhī*) and describe it as acrid and digestive, used against cold humours, nausea, cough, asthma and colic [@dymock1893]. A compilation of the Sanskrit medical works published in Calcutta in 1877 adds the household detail: ginger with salt before meals as a carminative said to clear the tongue and throat and sharpen the appetite, the fresh juice with honey for catarrh and cough, ginger juice in milk as a snuff for headache, and compound preparations such as *samaśarkarā cūrṇa*, six spices in rising proportions, dried ginger the largest, with an equal weight of sugar, for dyspepsia and poor appetite [@dutt1877]. A recent Ayurvedic review traces the same uses through the classical texts to formulations still in use [@singh2025].

### China

Confucius, according to the *Analects*, "was never without ginger when he ate" [@legge1861]. Chinese materia medica treated fresh ginger (*shēngjiāng*) and dried ginger (*gānjiāng*) as different drugs; Li Shizhen's sixteenth-century *Bencao gangmu* gives them separate articles [@stuart1911]. An English summary of that tradition records fresh ginger as used to stimulate digestion, quiet nausea and check cough, and as thought to counteract mushroom poisoning, and dried ginger for a wider range including haemorrhage [@stuart1911]. A recent review of the tradition distinguishes fresh, dried, baked and carbonised ginger, each credited with different strengths, while noting that their main chemical constituents are nearly identical [@zhang2024].

### The Islamic world

The Qur'an names *zanjabīl* among the drinks of paradise, a cup "mixed with the water of Zenjebil" in George Sale's eighteenth-century translation [@yule1903]. Galen recommended ginger for complaints arising from cold humours, and Ibn Sina and the Arabic and Persian physicians followed the Greeks while enlarging on its reputation as an aphrodisiac [@dymock1893].

### What this section could not consult

Three bodies of evidence that belong here were not read directly: Dioscorides, Galen and the Arabic pharmacologists in full modern editions, the Sanskrit texts beyond one translation and one nineteenth-century compilation, and the Chinese classical formularies. They are cited here only through the nineteenth- and early twentieth-century scholars who read them, and the section should be taken as a guide to them rather than a substitute. <!-- framing -->

## 6. Europe to the Americas

In medieval Europe ginger was a luxury, a medicine and a seasoning at once; in the sixteenth century it crossed the Atlantic; in the nineteenth it became a drink; and in 1930 its name became attached to a mass poisoning. <!-- framing -->

### The medieval trade

Ginger reached England early: it is named in Anglo-Saxon leechbooks of the eleventh century [@flueckiger1879]. It appears in customs tariffs at Acre about 1173, Barcelona in 1221, Marseilles in 1228 and Paris in 1296, and in thirteenth- and fourteenth-century England it was, after pepper, the commonest spice, at an average of nearly 1s. 7d. a pound, "about the price of a sheep" [@flueckiger1879]. Italian merchants of the fourteenth century knew three grades by their origin: *belledi* (from the Arabic for "country", common ginger), *colombino* (from Quilon on the Malabar coast) and *micchino* (by way of Mecca) [@flueckiger1879]. A Parisian household book of about 1393 prices them: a pound of colombin ginger powder at eleven sous, a quarter-pound of Mecca ginger at five, the Mecca being whiter, softer and dearer [@pichon1846]. Marco Polo saw ginger growing in China and India, and named the Quilon kind [@flueckiger1879; @yule1903]. Archaeology now confirms the documents: ginger was among the spices recovered from *Gribshunden*, the Danish royal flagship that sank in 1495 on its way to a political summit [@larsson2023].

### Ginger in humoral medicine

The reasoning behind the medieval demand is set out in the pharmacology texts of the period. Health was a balance of four humours, each hot or cold and wet or dry; ginger, classed as hot and dry, was used to correct a cold and wet constitution and diseases attributed to cold [@nam2014]. The classification was precise: an Irish materia medica rates ginger hot in the third degree and wet in the second [@ocuinnnd], Gerard's 1597 *Herball* hot and dry in the third degree, with candied green ginger moister and reputed to provoke desire [@gerard1597]. The degrees were not fixed: Culpeper's herbal, in its 1835 edition, puts ginger hot and dry only in the second degree, crediting it with helping digestion, warming the stomach and expelling wind, and repeatedly adds a little ginger to purging herbs so that "the stomach will not loath it" [@culpeper1835]. That use of ginger to stop a medicine causing nausea is an early form of the use the modern trials support best. <!-- synthesis --> Imported spices were valued above cheap local remedies, and partly for being exotic [@nam2014]. The same logic shaped demand: preferences for ginger differed between northern and southern Europe in ways that reflected humoral principles [@aram2015], and a French physician's treatise of 1702, translated into English in 1745, still judged it good for the old, the phlegmatic and the slow to digest, and "pernicious" to the young of hot and bilious constitution [@lemery1745].

### Across the Atlantic

Ginger was carried to the Americas in the sixteenth century; the nineteenth-century account credits Francisco de Mendoza with taking it from the East Indies to New Spain [@flueckiger1879]. It flourished on Hispaniola and Puerto Rico in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, found more buyers in England and the Low Countries than in Castile, and fitted poorly into the Spanish fleet system, which encouraged privateering and contraband [@aram2015]. Gerard reported that English raiders at Santo Domingo dug it up growing wild [@gerard1597]. The shift was already visible in 1596, when the Dutch traveller Linschoten wrote that most of the ginger reaching Spain came from the Cape Verde islands, São Tomé, Brazil and Santo Domingo, and little from Portuguese India, whose ginger he judged better but too costly to ship [@watt1893]. By the eighteenth century fresh ginger was eaten in the Antilles as a salad, and preserved ginger was carried at sea against scurvy [@lemery1745]. Jamaica ginger became the grade most esteemed in the London market [@flueckiger1879; @kings1898], and scraped ginger was often bleached with the fumes of burning sulphur or with chlorinated lime, so that some of it looked whitewashed [@watt1893]; edible ginger varieties are recorded as transplanted to the Caribbean by Spanish colonists in the sixteenth century [@fitzwilliam2019].

### From spice to drink

Ginger beer, ginger wine and ginger ale became a large part of the English mineral-water trade in the nineteenth century, and bottled ginger ale from Belfast was a fashionable drink across the United States by 1888 [@dymock1893]. The household ginger beer of the period was often fermented with a gelatinous "ginger-beer plant", which the botanist Marshall Ward showed in 1891 to be a symbiotic association of a new yeast and a new bacterium, comparable to a lichen [@ward1891].

### "Jake"

The episode most often associated with ginger in American history was not caused by ginger. In 1930, during Prohibition, an illicit extract of Jamaica ginger, drunk to get around the Prohibition laws, was adulterated with tri-ortho-cresyl phosphate [@morgan1976]. Thousands were poisoned [@morgan1978]; one account puts the number with partial paralysis at 50,000 [@morgan1982]. The damage to the spinal cord and nerves was only partly reversible, and survivors examined 47 years later still showed spasticity [@morgan1978]. The gait it left, "jake leg", was sung about on a dozen commercial recordings by Southern musicians [@morgan1976]. The neurotoxicity of the compound had been recognised in France before the epidemic, and the cause was identified by an American pharmacologist and chemist soon after it [@petroianu2016].

## 7. Why people spice food

One influential answer to why cuisines use spices at all comes from evolutionary biology, and ginger is part of the dataset behind it. <!-- framing -->

The antimicrobial hypothesis holds that spices are used because they inhibit or kill food-spoilage microorganisms. Its authors tabulated 43 spices in 4578 meat-based recipes from 93 traditional cookbooks covering 36 countries, and found that as mean annual temperature rose, so did the proportion of recipes containing spices, the number of spices per recipe and the use of the most potent antibacterial spices [@billing1998]. They considered four alternatives (that spices supply nutrients, disguise spoilage, promote cooling by sweating, or do nothing useful) and found none well supported [@billing1998; @sherman1999]. A recent review of ginger in British food history argues against the masking explanation in particular, and traces ginger's disappearance from savoury English cooking after the seventeenth century, as spiced food went out of fashion, while it survived in sweet baking [@spence2023].

Ginger's compounds do inhibit microbes in the laboratory (section 13), which fits the hypothesis. The medieval sources of section 6 give a different stated reason for spicing food, the balancing of humours, and the Roman spiced salts were credited with aiding digestion [@apicius1922]. The reasons people give and the reasons a practice persists need not be the same, and a correlation across cookbooks cannot separate them. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->
