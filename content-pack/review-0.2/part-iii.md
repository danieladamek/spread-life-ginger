# Part III. Health: the human evidence

<!-- section: 8-nausea-and-vomiting -->
## 8. Nausea and vomiting

Nausea is the use for which ginger has been tested longest and most often, and the answer depends on the cause of the nausea. This section takes motion, pregnancy, surgery and chemotherapy in turn, in the order in which they were first studied. Throughout, nausea and vomiting are kept apart, because ginger often moves one and not the other, and any amount of ginger mentioned is the test article of a study, not a recommendation. <!-- framing -->

<!-- figure: fig-evidence -->

### Motion sickness: where the trials began

The modern clinical literature on ginger starts with a 1982 letter in *The Lancet*. In a double-blind comparison, students who were highly susceptible to motion sickness took powdered ginger, dimenhydrinate or placebo before sitting in a rotating chair; a later tabulation of the study reports that those given ginger lasted longest in the chair [@mowrey1982; @bestbets2011]. The result was striking enough to start a line of replication.

The replications divided. On the open sea, 80 naval cadets unaccustomed to heavy weather took 1 g of powdered rhizome or placebo; ginger reduced vomiting and cold sweating significantly, but its effects on nausea and vertigo did not reach significance [@grontved1988]. In the laboratory the effect did not hold. A NASA-supported study found three doses of ginger no better than placebo, while dimenhydrinate, promethazine and scopolamine protected [@wood1988]. A second rotating-chair study with 28 volunteers found that neither powdered nor fresh ginger gave any protection, and that powdered ginger did not change gastric emptying [@stewart1991]. A German study recorded eye movements under vestibular and visual stimulation and found ginger no different from placebo, whereas dimenhydrinate damped the response; its authors concluded that ginger does not act on the balance system in the way conventional motion-sickness drugs do and that any benefit more likely arises in the stomach [@holtmann1989]. The one later mechanistic study, using a rotating visual field in thirteen volunteers, found that ginger reduced nausea along with abnormal gastric rhythm and the rise in vasopressin [@lien2003].

The European regulator nonetheless accepts powdered ginger for preventing nausea and vomiting in motion sickness as a well-established use, the only well-established use in its monograph, and limits it to adults [@emamonograph2025]. A 2005 review read the same trials more sceptically and found the motion-sickness evidence unconvincing [@chrubasik2005]. The two positions rest on the same handful of small studies weighed differently. <!-- synthesis -->

### Pregnancy

Pregnancy is where ginger's evidence is strongest. The first randomised trial, in 1991, was in hyperemesis gravidarum, the severe form of pregnancy sickness: 27 women completed a double-blind crossover of powdered ginger against placebo, 19 of them preferred the ginger period, and relief scores were significantly better with ginger [@fischer-rasmussen1991]. A ten-year run of trials followed. In 70 women at or before 17 weeks, ginger reduced both nausea scores and vomiting episodes over four days compared with placebo [@vutyavanich2001]. In 120 women given a standardised extract, nausea fell from the first day of treatment and retching less so, with no significant effect on vomiting [@willetts2003]. In an equivalence trial in 291 women, ginger and vitamin B6 reduced nausea, retching and vomiting to an equivalent extent over three weeks [@smith2004].

The pooled analyses agree on nausea and part company on vomiting. A 2005 systematic review of six double-blind trials with 675 participants found four favouring ginger over placebo and two showing it as effective as vitamin B6 [@borrelli2005]. A 2014 meta-analysis of twelve trials involving 1278 women found ginger significantly better than placebo for nausea but not for the number of vomiting episodes [@viljoen2014]. The 2022 umbrella review ranked the antiemetic effect in pregnancy as its best-supported finding, with a large effect and a high GRADE rating [@crichton2022]. Two later appraisals are more cautious: a 2024 umbrella review rated the seven meta-analyses it found, which drew on 22 overlapping studies, as of critically low to low quality [@tiani2024], and the Cochrane review of all interventions for early-pregnancy nausea judged the evidence for every intervention, ginger included, to be limited and of low quality [@matthews2015].

### Surgery

The surgical trials divide early and stay divided. In 60 women having major gynaecological surgery, ginger produced fewer recorded episodes of nausea than placebo, similar to metoclopramide [@bone1990]. In 108 women having laparoscopic gynaecological surgery, ginger at two doses was no better than placebo, and nausea and vomiting rose slightly with dose [@arfeen1995]. The first systematic review, in 2000, found the pooled reduction in postoperative nausea not significant [@ernst2000]; a 2006 meta-analysis of five trials with 363 patients found relative risks of 0.69 for nausea and vomiting and 0.61 for vomiting, favouring ginger [@chaiyakunapruk2006]. In 239 women having caesarean section under spinal anaesthesia, ginger reduced the number of nausea episodes during surgery but did not reduce the proportion of women who had nausea [@kalava2013].

### Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy has the largest trials and the least consistent results. In 60 chemotherapy cycles in children and young adults with bone sarcoma, all given standard antiemetics, ginger capsules reduced moderate to severe acute nausea from 93.3% of cycles to 55.6%, with similar reductions in vomiting and in delayed symptoms [@pillai2010]. In 576 adults analysed in a multicentre trial, all ginger doses reduced the severity of acute nausea on the first day [@ryan2012]. A trial in 162 adults focused on delayed symptoms found no difference from placebo in any primary or secondary measure [@zick2008b]. A 2023 trial of a standardised extract in 103 adults found benefit in nausea-related quality of life and delayed nausea, and none in acute symptoms [@crichton2023]. The reviews mirror the trials: a 2013 review of seven studies counted three positive, two positive with caveats and two negative [@marx2013], and a 2022 meta-analysis of 23 trials found no overall difference in the incidence of acute or delayed nausea or vomiting, with a reduction in acute vomiting only in one dosing subgroup [@choi2022].

### What the trials share

The weaknesses are common to all four settings. Of 109 randomised trials reviewed in 2020, only 43 (39.4%) were judged to provide high-quality evidence, with small samples and unstandardised outcome measures as the usual faults [@anh2020]. The test articles vary from fresh rhizome to powdered root to standardised extracts, and few trials report how much of any compound they delivered. <!-- framing -->

## 9. Pain, inflammation and metabolic outcomes

The second large body of trials tests ginger as an anti-inflammatory, an idea with roots in the laboratory work of the 1970s and 1980s described in section 11. The pattern of this section is consistent: pooled effects usually favour ginger, and the certainty attached to them is usually low. <!-- framing -->

### Osteoarthritis

Two trials from 2000 and 2001 set the terms. A Danish double-blind crossover compared a ginger extract with ibuprofen and placebo in osteoarthritis of the hip or knee; ibuprofen ranked above ginger, which ranked above placebo, but a significant effect of ginger could be shown only with exploratory statistics in the first treatment period [@bliddal2000]. A six-week American trial in 261 patients with knee osteoarthritis found that 63% of the extract group and 50% of the placebo group met its pain-response criterion, an effect its authors called moderate; the extract combined *Zingiber officinale* with *Alpinia galanga*, and gastrointestinal side effects, mostly mild, were reported by 59 patients on the extract against 21 on placebo [@altman2001]. A 2015 meta-analysis of five placebo-controlled trials with 593 patients found a small but significant reduction in pain (standardised mean difference −0.30) and noted that most trials lacked an adequate intention-to-treat analysis [@bartels2014]. The 2022 umbrella review rates the effect as small, with high certainty [@crichton2022].

### Other pain

A 2011 systematic review of eight trials with 481 participants found tentative support for an effect on pain and judged the evidence insufficient overall [@terry2011]. Muscle soreness after eccentric exercise was reduced by about a quarter, compared with placebo, by both raw and heat-treated ginger taken for eleven days, in two trials of 34 and 40 volunteers [@black2010]. For period pain, a 2009 trial in 150 students found ginger as effective as mefenamic acid and ibuprofen [@ozgoli2009]; meta-analyses in 2015 and 2024 found ginger better than placebo for pain intensity [@daily2015; @moshfeghinia2024], the later one with high heterogeneity, sparse reporting of safety and no difference from non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [@moshfeghinia2024]. For migraine there is one double-blind trial, in which sixty adults given ginger extract on top of intravenous ketoprofen in an emergency room responded better at one, one and a half and two hours [@martins2018].

### Blood sugar, lipids and inflammatory markers

Most metabolic trials measure laboratory values over weeks. Two meta-analyses in type 2 diabetes, each of ten trials, report lower fasting glucose and glycated haemoglobin, with a pooled HbA1c difference of −1.00 in one [@zhu2018] and −0.57 in the other, where heterogeneity was 88.6% [@ebrahimzadeh2022]. They disagree about lipids: the first reports improvement [@zhu2018], the second no significant effect on any lipid fraction [@ebrahimzadeh2022]. An older trial in patients with coronary disease found no change in lipids or blood sugar after three months of powdered ginger [@bordia1997]. For inflammatory markers, a 2025 dose–response meta-analysis of 29 trials reports reductions in C-reactive protein, tumour necrosis factor α and interleukin-6, with high heterogeneity [@rjabi2025]; a 2026 umbrella review of 36 publications reaches a favourable overall verdict [@musazadeh2026]; and a 2025 review of meta-analyses agrees on direction while pointing to heterogeneity of 98.1% in one analysis and possible bias in blinding in another [@paudel2025]. The umbrella review grades the glycaemic effect anywhere from none to very large at very low to moderate certainty, and the lipid effect as small at very low certainty [@crichton2022].

None of the sources read reports an effect of ginger on a disease outcome, such as a diabetic complication, a heart attack or a joint replacement; the evidence concerns symptoms and surrogate measures over weeks to months. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

## 10. Safety, interactions and pregnancy

Ginger is a food, and evidence about harm comes mostly from trials designed to detect benefit and from observational studies of people who chose to use it. This section reports what has been looked for and not found, which is different from what has been ruled out. <!-- framing -->

### What the regulator concluded

The European Union monograph, revised in 2025, is the most formal assessment available. It lists common gastrointestinal effects (stomach upset, belching, indigestion, heartburn and nausea) and hypersensitivity of unknown frequency, records no known interactions, and has no reported cases of overdose [@emamonograph2025]. It sets age limits for every use, and it does not recommend the well-established motion-sickness use under 18 [@emamonograph2025].

### Pregnancy

The trials did not detect harm: no adverse effect on pregnancy outcome in the 70-woman trial [@vutyavanich2001], normal birthweights, gestational ages and malformation rates in the 120-woman extract trial [@willetts2003], and no significant risk of miscarriage in the 2014 meta-analysis, though with a confidence interval from 0.65 to 15.11 that is too wide to exclude one [@viljoen2014]. Observational studies extend the numbers. A Canadian cohort followed 187 pregnancies exposed to ginger in the first trimester and found no difference from a comparison group in malformations or other outcomes [@portnoi2003]. The Norwegian Mother and Child Cohort compared 1020 women who reported using ginger with more than 67,000 who did not and found no increased risk of malformations, stillbirth or perinatal death, preterm birth, low birthweight or low Apgar score [@heitmann2012].

Regulators have read the same evidence conservatively. The European monograph describes a moderate amount of data, 300 to 1000 pregnancy outcomes, showing no malformative or toxic effect on the fetus, notes that animal data on reproductive toxicity are insufficient, and advises as a precaution that use in pregnancy is preferably avoided; it does not recommend use while breast-feeding, for lack of data [@emamonograph2025].

### Bleeding and anticoagulants

The concern began in the laboratory and in small studies of platelets. Seven Danish women who ate 5 g of raw ginger a day for a week showed a fall in serum thromboxane [@srivastava1989], and in human blood in vitro several ginger constituents inhibited platelet activation more potently than aspirin [@nurtjahja-tjendraputra2003]. Studies in people taking dried ginger did not reproduce an effect: 2 g had no effect on bleeding time or platelet function in eight healthy men [@lumb1994], and 4 g daily for three months did not alter platelet aggregation in patients with coronary disease, though a single 10 g dose did [@bordia1997]. In twelve healthy men, ginger at recommended doses did not change clotting or the handling of warfarin [@jiang2005]. Against that, a prospective study of 171 patients on warfarin found ginger among the complementary products associated with self-reported bleeding [@shalansky2007]. A 2000 review found no evidence of a warfarin–ginger interaction in the literature to that date [@vaes2000], and the regulator lists none [@emamonograph2025]. The evidence is reassuring for dried ginger in healthy people and thin for people on long-term anticoagulation, the group in which a small effect would matter. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

## 11. How it might work, and the bioavailability problem

Mechanisms for ginger's effects have been proposed for fifty years, nearly all from cells, isolated tissue or animals. Whether they operate in a person depends on how much of which compound reaches which tissue, and that is where the account becomes difficult. <!-- framing -->

### The anti-inflammatory chemistry

The anti-inflammatory idea rests on enzyme work. Ginger's inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis was described in the early 1970s and repeatedly confirmed, and was later extended to leukotriene synthesis through 5-lipoxygenase, a combination that distinguishes it from ordinary non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [@grzanna2005]. Gingerols and related diarylheptanoids were identified as the active inhibitors of both enzyme pathways in the 1980s and early 1990s [@kiuchi1992].

### The gut and serotonin receptors

For nausea, the best-supported mechanism is inhibition of the 5-HT3 receptor, the target of standard antiemetic drugs. In 2006, 6-, 8- and 10-gingerol and 6-shogaol all inhibited 5-HT3 receptor activity in cultured cells and contractions of guinea-pig gut [@abdel-aziz2006]. In 2013 ginger extracts, 6-gingerol and 6-shogaol inhibited human 5-HT3 receptors, in cells and in human gut nerve tissue, at low micromolar concentrations and by a non-competitive mechanism, binding elsewhere than the drugs do [@walstab2013]. In guinea-pig tissue the effect on 5-HT3 and muscarinic M3 receptors was weak, and 5-HT4 receptors appeared not to be involved [@pertz2011]. Ginger compounds also hastened the movement of a test meal through the gut in mice, comparably to metoclopramide [@yamahara1990].

Human studies point to the stomach. Ginger capsules shortened gastric half-emptying time from 26.7 to 13.1 minutes in 24 healthy volunteers [@wu2008] and from 16.1 to 12.3 minutes in 11 patients with functional dyspepsia [@hu2010], with no change in symptoms in either study. The motion-sickness work points the same way, finding no effect on the balance system [@holtmann1989].

<!-- figure: fig-gastric -->

### Pungency and TRPV1

Gingerols were identified in 2002 as a new class of agonists at the capsaicin receptor TRPV1, with potency rising with the length of the side chain [@dedov2002]. Later work found shogaols more potent than gingerols at the receptor, and 10-shogaol active without being pungent [@iwasaki2006], and showed that gingerol, shogaol and zingerone depend on the same two residues in the channel that capsaicin does [@yin2019].

### What reaches the blood

The first human pharmacokinetic study gave 27 volunteers single doses from 100 mg to 2.0 g. No participant had detectable free 6-, 8- or 10-gingerol or 6-shogaol in plasma; they appeared as glucuronide and sulfate conjugates, peaked at about an hour and were cleared with half-lives under two hours [@zick2008]. A study in asthma patients taking ginger extract for 56 days found about one-third of 6-gingerol reduced to gingerdiols and more than 90% of 6-shogaol converted to other metabolites [@zhang2022]. Laboratory and mouse work traces the low exposure to rapid first-pass conjugation [@mukkavilli2017].

<!-- figure: fig-cmax -->

The receptor studies use micromolar concentrations of the free compounds; the human studies find conjugates and metabolites in blood. Two readings fit the evidence. Ginger may act mainly on the stomach and gut wall, which meet the free compounds before the liver does; or the metabolites may themselves be active. The sources read here do not settle which. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

### Beyond the gut

Laboratory studies attribute many other activities to ginger, among them inhibition of *Helicobacter pylori* at microgram-per-millilitre concentrations [@mahady2003] and antioxidant and anticancer effects in cells and animals [@ali2007]. By the scope of this review these are noted, not argued: none has a body of human trials behind it. <!-- framing -->
