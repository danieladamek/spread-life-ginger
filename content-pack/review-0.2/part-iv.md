# Part IV. The plant itself

## 12. The making of gingerols

How ginger builds its pungent compounds has been worked out over fifty years by three different methods: feeding labelled precursors to the plant, measuring enzyme activities and gene expression, and finally expressing candidate genes in microbes. Each stage confirmed the outline of the one before. <!-- framing -->

### The outline from labelled precursors, 1976–1981

The first pathway came from feeding isotope-labelled compounds to whole plants and rhizome slices. In 1976 the evidence pointed to dihydroferulate, built from the amino acid phenylalanine, condensing with malonate and hexanoate in what the authors called a "biological Claisen" reaction, followed by reduction of the product to 6-gingerol [@denniff1976]. The fuller study refined it: phenylalanine is made into ferulic acid, which condenses with malonate and hexanoate to give 6-dehydrogingerdione; hexanoate goes in intact; and the dione is reduced in two steps to 6-gingerol [@denniff1980]. The length of the gingerol side chain, and so the difference between 6-, 8- and 10-gingerol, follows from which fatty acid joins the condensation. <!-- synthesis -->

### Enzymes and genes, 2006–2018

Enzyme assays then confirmed the front of the pathway. Crude extracts of ginger and turmeric leaf, shoot and rhizome carried the activities of the phenylpropanoid enzymes, but in ginger a thioesterase destroyed the feruloyl-CoA precursor in the test tube and hid any gingerol-forming activity [@ramirez-ahumada2006]. Gene-expression surveys narrowed the candidates to type III polyketide synthases enriched in the rhizome [@koo2013]. In 2018 a transcriptome study, stating that the pathway had still not been verified in ginger, confirmed that rhizomes are the main site of gingerol accumulation, found accumulation beginning early in rhizome development, and identified five pathway genes upregulated at that stage [@jiang2018].

### The genome and the regulators, 2021–2024

The genome assemblies turned candidates into a proposed route of twelve enzyme families running from phenylalanine to gingerol [@li2021], and identified transcription factors that may control it [@cheng2021]. The 2024 assembly showed that two of them, ZoMYB106 and ZobHLH148, bind the promoters of two caffeoyl-CoA O-methyltransferase genes in the pathway and switch them on [@chen2024].

### The key enzyme, 2026

The step the 1980 work had inferred, the condensation that makes 6-dehydrogingerdione, has now been shown with ginger's own enzymes. Expressing pairs of ginger polyketide synthases in bacteria supplied with feruloyl- and hexanoyl-CoA, a 2026 study found that one, ZoPKS2, makes 6-dehydrogingerdione as its major product, while its partner ZoPKS1 is a curcumin synthase of the kind turmeric uses [@pena2026]. The authors note that genes for gingeroid biosynthesis in ginger had not previously been known [@pena2026]. The reductions that turn the dione into gingerol remain to be shown enzyme by enzyme in the sources read here.

The aromatic oil is better mapped than the pungent fraction. A single 2012 study identified 25 monoterpene and 18 sesquiterpene synthases from ginger and turmeric and characterised more than twenty of them [@koo2012], and the enzyme that turns geraniol into the citrus-scented citral has been cloned [@iijima2014].

## 13. What the compounds do for the plant

The greenhouse team asked a direct question: what are these compounds for, from the plant's side? The literature answers it only indirectly, and this section is short for that reason. <!-- framing -->

### Where the compounds sit

The gingerols are concentrated in the stele and cortex of the rhizome [@torrecampo2026], tissues riddled with oil cells and cavities that multiply as the rhizome matures [@liu2020]. Accumulation begins early in rhizome development [@jiang2018]. A storage organ that is packed with reactive, bitter or pungent compounds from its early stages is the pattern ecologists associate with chemical defence, but placement is not proof of purpose. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

### Evidence of activity against microbes

Bioassay-guided fractionation of an African land race identified 6-, 8- and 10-gingerol and 6-gingerdiol as its main antifungal compounds, though the organisms tested were 13 human pathogens [@ficker2003]. Ginger's essential oil fully inhibited growth and spore germination of a taro pathogen, *Phytophthora colocasiae*, at 1250 ppm in the laboratory [@kalhoro2022]. Against ginger's own bacterial wilt pathogen, *Ralstonia solanacearum*, a ginger extract in which 393 compounds were identified was bactericidal in a dose-dependent way, damaged the pathogen's membranes and slowed the disease on ginger plants [@zhang2022b]. Most directly, when ginger was infected with *Ralstonia*, the composition of its root exudates changed, and the exudates that increased lowered the disease index from 77.50% to 40.00%, which the authors read as ginger secreting antibacterial compounds in its own defence [@dang2023]. None of these studies isolates the gingerols as the active defence in a living plant.

### The plant's enemies and allies

Ginger is susceptible to at least 24 pathogens, among them viruses, bacteria, fungi and nematodes, and the *Pythium* water moulds are the most damaging because they can rot the rhizome at any stage, including in storage [@le2014]. *Pythium gracile*, the first species reported on ginger, was described in India in 1907, and 15 species have since been implicated [@le2014]. A comparison of healthy and rotting plants concluded that the assembly of the whole root microbial community, not any single microbe, determined plant health, and linked that assembly to particular metabolites, lipids most of all [@wang2024]. Inside healthy rhizomes, four endophytic bacterial strains have been isolated, one a *Pseudomonas* that makes a plant hormone and other growth-promoting compounds [@jasim2013]. A wild relative resistant to soft rot, *Zingiber zerumbet*, switches on a set of defence and signalling genes when challenged by *Pythium* [@kavitha2008].

What these studies establish is that ginger's tissues and exudates can suppress microbes and that an infected plant changes what it releases. What none of them shows is that the gingerols in a living rhizome protect it from the organisms that attack it. That remains a reasonable hypothesis, untested in the sources read here. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- framing -->

## 14. Growing ginger under glass

This section gathers what the physiological and horticultural literature says about heat, water, light and growing systems, as background for anyone growing ginger in a greenhouse. It is not a cultivation guide. <!-- framing -->

### Heat, water and light

Heat and standing water are each damaging and worse together. Holding plants during rhizome growth at day temperatures of 28, 33 or 38 °C, with or without waterlogging, showed that both stresses irreversibly reduced photosynthetic pigments and carbon assimilation, and that photosynthesis was more sensitive to the combination than to either alone [@liu2022]. Combined high temperature and intense light caused leaves to curl, yellow and wilt, with falling chlorophyll and rising markers of membrane damage [@metabolites2023]. Light quality matters as well as quantity: supplementing the green band of sunlight raised ginger's photosynthetic rate and the efficiency of photosystem II [@lv2022]. Day length steers the plant between leaf growth and rhizome swelling, with short days favouring the rhizome in the Japanese cultivars tested [@adaniya1989].

### The rhizome's development

As the rhizome enlarges, its levels of four plant hormones (zeatin, gibberellic acid, indole acetic acid and jasmonic acid) rise in step with its diameter [@ren2023]. Gingerols begin to accumulate early in that development [@jiang2018], and oil cells multiply as it matures [@liu2020].

### Soil, soilless and hydroponic systems

Ginger has been grown successfully out of soil. In a Florida comparison, a greenhouse hydroponic system gave higher yields and better-quality rhizomes than field growing, with lower costs for disease, insect and weed control [@rafie2003]. In a Malaysian fertigation trial of organic substrates, plants in 100% coir dust grew best and yielded most of five substrate mixes tested [@suhaimi2012]. In the short temperate season of the US Delmarva Peninsula, tissue-culture transplants with three or more shoots gave the highest rhizome yields, up to 648.3 g per plant in a high tunnel in the first year [@marsh2021]. None of the sources read compares the gingerol content of hydroponic and soil-grown ginger directly, which is the comparison the greenhouse team is running.

### Disease under cover

Protected growing does not keep disease out. Ginger wilt, caused by *Ralstonia pseudosolanacearum*, was first detected in Minnesota in 2023 in high-tunnel ginger; it spreads through contaminated seed rhizome, soil and water [@minnesota2024]. Clean planting material matters for a crop propagated from its own rhizomes, which is the reason tissue-culture propagation was developed [@ma2006].
